How the League Works

Learn, diagnose, design, build, test, revise, and share.

The League combines a five-lesson introduction with a structured student design process that can be used in classrooms, tutoring, small groups, or afterschool clubs.

Phase 1

Begin with five introductory lessons

1

Humans learn; AI generates

Students compare human learning, understanding, experience, and creativity with the pattern-based outputs generated by AI systems.

2

Diagnose the challenge

Students move from broad statements such as “I am bad at math” to a specific, evidence-supported description of where learning breaks down.

3

Match the strategy

Students connect challenges to strategies such as retrieval practice, self-explanation, worked examples, feedback, chunking, and reflection.

4

Design the AI interaction

Students define the AI’s role, sequence, feedback rules, limits, safety protections, and responsibilities.

5

Test, revise, and share

Students test prototypes with users, evaluate whether the tool supports thinking, revise it, and explain the learning-science rationale.

What students produce

A responsible AI-supported learning aid

  • A clear learning goal
  • A specific, evidence-supported learning challenge
  • An analysis of task components
  • An evidence-based learning strategy
  • An AI interaction aligned with that strategy
  • Safety, privacy, and accuracy safeguards
  • Test evidence and a revision record
  • A presentation of strengths, limits, and lessons learned
What advisors do

Facilitate the process

  • Teach or facilitate the five lessons
  • Help students clarify learning challenges
  • Support team roles and collaboration
  • Review platform, policy, safety, and privacy requirements
  • Approve user-testing plans
  • Provide feedback without taking over student work
  • Document progress and implementation feedback
Flexible formats

Designed for different school settings

One-on-one tutoring

A teacher guides one student through the lessons and design process, with peer or family feedback added where possible.

Small-group club

Students interview one another, work in pairs or teams, rotate roles, and test prototypes with peers.

Classroom integration

The five lessons can be taught in class, followed by extended project work during club meetings or independent work time.

Virtual club

Students from multiple schools can meet online for interviews, design critiques, tool testing, and presentations.

Lunch or short-cycle club

Advisors can use shorter mini-lessons and structured work sessions across several days.

Boot camp or showcase day

Students can gather for an intensive build, testing, feedback, and presentation experience.

Success looks like

Students can explain both the learning and the technology.

A successful project is not simply a functioning chatbot. Students should be able to explain the learner’s goal, the diagnosed challenge, the learning strategy, the AI’s behavior, the evidence from testing, the revisions made, and the tool’s remaining limitations.
Decision points

Questions students answer at each stage

StageCore questionStudent evidence
LearnWhat does meaningful learning require?Concept map, discussion, or reflection
DiagnoseWhat exactly is difficult for the learner?Interview notes and challenge statement
DesignWhich learning strategy best matches the need?Strategy rationale and interaction plan
BuildHow should the AI behave?Prompt, storyboard, or prototype
TestWhat happens when someone uses it?Observation notes and feedback
ReviseWhat should change and why?Version comparison and revision log
ShareWhat did we learn and what are the limits?Presentation, poster, or design notebook
Important: The League evaluates the quality of the learning diagnosis, strategy match, AI interaction, safety review, testing evidence, and revision—not simply whether a chatbot produces fluent responses.
