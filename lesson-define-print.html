Session 2 · Define

Find the real learning problem

Back to session

Session purpose

Move from a broad learning difficulty to one specific, observable breakdown that a team can realistically design for.

Student stage: Define

Materials

  • student Empathize notes
  • sample task or work attempt when appropriate
  • Task Map worksheet or student stage
  • paper step cards
  • timer

Before the session

Try the activity yourself, read the short explanation, and decide what adaptations your students may need.

Facilitator preparation

Try the student activity

  • Try narrowing a broad learning problem yourself. The point is to feel why “I don’t understand this subject” is too large to design for.
  • Choose one, then check what you notice.
  • Choose an everyday task you know well and write its first four meaningful steps. Notice how much hidden knowledge you normally skip when you already know the task.

Why this matters

  • Where did you want to jump to a solution?
  • What information did you realize you were missing?
  • What might a student need modeled rather than merely explained?
  • Task decomposition makes hidden steps visible. A breakdown is your best evidence-based hypothesis about where the process changes—not a label placed on the learner. Access barriers can also look like learning problems, so check those first.

Plan your support

  • Pick one example task you can decompose aloud. Be ready to say, “We are looking for the first place the process changes, not blaming the learner.”

Student meeting: 1 × 45-minute meeting

Reopen the Empathy Map 0–5 minutes

What to do

  1. Open the student’s Empathy Map from Stage 1.
  2. Ask which parts came directly from the interview and which parts are still questions.
  3. Choose one real STEM task to map. Do not ask students to explain the cause yet.

Student website step: Student Step 1 · Start here

Students produce: a learner goal plus two pieces of empathy evidence.

Map the learning task in sequence 5–17 minutes

What to do

  1. Model a familiar task in 4–6 steps. For each step, name what the learner does and what knowledge, representation, or decision the step requires.
  2. Open the Learning Breakdown Mapper. Students will see one task step at a time and a visual map of the whole sequence. Have them mark each step as working, needing help, where it gets hard, or still uncertain. Students add one task step at a time and mark each step as working, needs help, gets hard, or not sure.
  3. Require a short piece of evidence at important steps: what did the learner say, do, write, or need help with?
  4. If the team cannot see the hidden steps, use the optional Task Breakdown Helper prompt. Treat the AI response as a draft to critique, then revise the Task Map yourselves.
  5. Look for the earliest meaningful change in performance rather than automatically choosing the final visible error.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

What to explain

Task decomposition

1-minute explainer: Complex performance depends on smaller knowledge, decisions, representations, and actions. The last wrong answer may occur after the real breakdown began.

Try saying: “We are going to rewind the task until we find the first step that changes.”

Example: A student gets slope wrong. The issue may be the formula—or earlier, reading axis intervals, identifying rise/run, or interpreting direction.

Watch for: Do not automatically treat the final visible error as the cause.

Students produce: a task map that records the learner action, needed knowledge, status, and evidence at each important step.

Name the first likely breakdown 17–27 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask: What can the learner already do without help?
  2. Find the earliest step where the evidence changes from success to confusion, guessing, or dependence on help.
  3. Write at least one alternative explanation for the same observation.
  4. Ask what evidence would distinguish the explanations.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

Students produce: one leading bottleneck hypothesis and at least one alternative explanation.

Check prerequisites, access, and direct evidence 27–35 minutes

What to do

  1. Look at the directions, language, visual layout, device demands, timing, and interaction format.
  2. Ask whether any of these could be preventing the learner from showing what they know.
  3. If access is contributing, decide whether the design should remove the barrier, support the learning process, or both.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

What to explain

Access versus understanding

1-minute explainer: Performance can be limited by how information or interaction is presented rather than by the target knowledge itself.

Try saying: “Before we teach something new, make sure the learner can access the instructions, representation, language, and interaction.”

Example: A learner may understand a graph concept but struggle because labels are tiny or directions are linguistically dense.

Watch for: Do not interpret an access barrier as low ability, low motivation, or a learning deficit.

Students produce: a note about access factors that must be addressed or ruled out.

Check scope and write the focused problem 35–42 minutes

What to do

  1. Run the mapper’s readiness check.
  2. If the result is Too broad, identify which part of the task or learner group can be narrowed.
  3. If it is Almost there, make the targeted revisions before proceeding.
  4. If it is Ready to design, complete: “The learner can already… The breakdown seems to happen when… Our evidence is… Therefore our project will focus on…”
  5. Name one observable change that would count as improvement.
  6. Write: “By the end of using our learning tool, the learner should be able to…” and complete it with one observable learning action.
  7. Write: “We will know because…” and name the task, explanation, decision, or performance that would count as evidence of learning.

Student website step: Student Step 4 · Write the learning target

Students produce: a small, evidence-based learning problem and observable target the facilitator approves.

Facilitator checkpoint 42–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask the team to complete three sentences: “The learner can already…,” “The breakdown seems to happen when…,” and “Our project will focus on…”.
  2. Ask what direct evidence supports that explanation.
  3. If the explanation is still broad or based mostly on assumption, send the team back one step.

Student website step: Student Step 5 · Check together

Students produce: a focused learning challenge that is ready for Ideate

Student meeting: 2 × 45-minute meetings

Session 1 — 45 minutes

Reopen the Empathy Map 0–5 minutes

What to do

  1. Open the student’s Empathy Map from Stage 1.
  2. Ask which parts came directly from the interview and which parts are still questions.
  3. Choose one real STEM task to map. Do not ask students to explain the cause yet.

Student website step: Student Step 1 · Start here

Students produce: a learner goal plus two pieces of empathy evidence.

Model task decomposition together 5–15 minutes

What to do

  1. Choose a familiar learning task that is not the team’s project.
  2. Ask what the learner does first, next, and after that.
  3. At each step, ask what knowledge or decision is required.
  4. Show how a final wrong answer may originate from an earlier step.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

What to explain

Task decomposition

1-minute explainer: A complex performance is made of smaller knowledge, decisions, and actions. The visible error may occur after the real breakdown.

Say it this way: “Let’s rewind the task until we find the first place the learner’s process changes.”

Example: A wrong slope answer may begin with miscounting intervals before the division step.

Watch for: Do not make the map so granular that students lose the overall learning goal.

Students produce: one shared example of a task broken into meaningful steps

Build the team’s task map 15–35 minutes

What to do

  1. Model a familiar task in 4–6 steps. Reveal one step at a time so students notice how much hidden thinking sits inside an apparently simple task. Say what the learner does at each step and what knowledge, representation, or decision is needed.
  2. Ask students to map their own task from the beginning rather than starting with the final error.
  3. Have them mark where the learner succeeds independently, succeeds with help, becomes inconsistent, or gets stuck.
  4. Encourage revision of earlier steps if students discover a missing prerequisite.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

What to explain

Task decomposition

Example: A student gets slope wrong. The issue may be the formula—or earlier, reading axis intervals, identifying rise/run, or interpreting direction.

Watch for: Do not automatically treat the final visible error as the cause.

Students produce: a task sequence that shows where performance changes.

Compare two bottleneck hypotheses 35–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Name the first likely breakdown.
  2. Name one plausible alternative explanation.
  3. For each, list what you would expect to see if it were true.
  4. Choose one quick observation, work sample, or mini-task that could distinguish them.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

Students produce: two testable explanations rather than one premature conclusion

Run a quick direct-evidence check 10–20 minutes

What to do

  1. Choose one tiny task related to the suspected bottleneck.
  2. Ask the learner to try it and explain their thinking, or inspect an existing work sample.
  3. Record what the learner does—not what the team expected them to do.
  4. Revise the bottleneck explanation if the evidence does not fit.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

What to explain

Use direct evidence before outside research

1-minute explainer: For the core League experience, interviews, observations, work samples, and a short task can be enough to test whether your proposed breakdown fits. Outside research is optional, not required.

Say it this way: “We need enough evidence to improve our explanation—not a research paper.”

Example: If you think the learner confuses graph points with intervals, ask them to work one short example and explain their counting.

Watch for: Do not require students to search for scholarly sources just to move forward.

Students produce: one piece of direct evidence that strengthens or weakens the hypothesis

Check whether the problem is small enough 20–30 minutes

What to do

  1. Run the mapper’s readiness check.
  2. If the result is Too broad, identify which part of the task or learner group can be narrowed.
  3. If it is Almost there, make the targeted revisions before proceeding.
  4. If it is Ready to design, complete: “The learner can already… The breakdown seems to happen when… Our evidence is… Therefore our project will focus on…”
  5. Name one observable change that would count as improvement.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Break down the task

Students produce: a small, evidence-based learning problem and observable target the facilitator approves.

Write the focused problem and success evidence 30–40 minutes

What to do

  1. Complete: “The learner can already…”
  2. Complete: “The breakdown seems to happen when…”
  3. Complete: “Therefore our project will focus on…”
  4. Name one observable behavior that would show progress.
  5. Write: “By the end of using our learning tool, the learner should be able to…” and complete it with one observable learning action.
  6. Write: “We will know because…” and name the task, explanation, decision, or performance that would count as evidence of learning.

Student website step: Student Step 4 · Write the learning target

Students produce: a focused problem statement and observable success measure

Facilitator checkpoint 40–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask the team to explain the task map without reading the form.
  2. Ask which observation most changed their thinking.
  3. Check that the project targets one learning process rather than a whole subject or unit.

Student website step: Student Step 5 · Check together

Students produce: facilitator agreement that the team is ready to Ideate

What students should have at the end

  • A step-by-step Task Map showing what the learner is trying to do, what already works, and where difficulty begins.
  • A focused learning problem stated as the team’s best current hypothesis—not a final answer.
  • A STEM connection for the project.
  • A learning objective: what the learner should be able to do differently.
  • Observable evidence that would show improvement.

Keep the project moving without adding another full session

  • Have students finish or revise the Learning Breakdown Map if the 45-minute session ended before the facilitator checkpoint.
  • Ask for one additional attempt, work sample, or observation when the proposed breakdown is still uncertain.
  • Carry the focused challenge and observable success target into Ideate.
