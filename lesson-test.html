Your goal

Your goal

Students will plan a focused test before using the prototype, observe learner behavior and learning evidence, revise from what happened, retest when possible, and prepare to explain and demonstrate the project at the sharing event.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

Testing is where the team's design assumptions meet actual use. A learner saying “I like it” is useful feedback, but it is not the same as evidence that the target learning behavior changed.

Share is the culmination of the design process. Students should not present only a polished product; they should tell the story of the challenge, what they learned about learning and AI, what they built, what testing revealed, what changed, and what remains uncertain.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Approve who may test the prototype, what permissions are required, what data may be recorded, and when testing should stop.
  • Review the team's prototype test question and target learning behavior.
  • Open the student Testing & Revision tool and Sharing Event Builder.
  • Prepare at least one backup testing or demonstration format in case technology fails.
  • Confirm the team knows the difference between a usability observation, a learning observation, and an unsupported claim.

Core materials

Student Test and Share pages; prototype; approved test task; observation notes; Testing & Revision tool; Sharing Event Builder; demo materials and backup.

Try this

Try this

Core activities are the minimum League experience. Keep these even when time is short. A lesson may span more than one meeting; do not remove evidence-gathering or testing simply to fit one session.

1

Plan before testing

10–15 min

Before anyone uses the prototype, students answer: Who should test it? What will they try? What will we observe? What would count as success? What result would make us redefine the problem rather than merely change the interface?

2

Use the lightest appropriate test

10–20 min

Begin with team self-test and peer test. Move to teacher-client review or learner testing only when appropriate and approved.

3

Observe behavior, not just opinions

15–20 min

Record where the person begins, hesitates, succeeds, asks for help, ignores a feature, changes strategy, or demonstrates the target learning behavior.

4

Revise from evidence

10–15 min

Choose one high-value revision linked directly to what was observed. Record why the team changed it.

5

Retest when possible

10–15 min

Use a comparable task or interaction so the team can see whether the change addressed the original issue.

6

Move into Share

20–30 min

Use Sharing Event Builder to prepare the challenge, what the team learned, design rationale, testing, revision, demo, limitations, and reflection. Rehearse one focused demonstration with a low-tech backup.

Just-in-time advisor learning

Open these only when you need the concept. Each is designed to take about two minutes and includes language you can immediately use with students.

2-minute background · Usability is not learning

Key idea: A tool can be easy, attractive, or enjoyable without changing what a learner knows or can do. Testing should examine both usability and the target learning action.

Say to students: “Ask what the learner could do after or during the interaction—not only whether they liked it.”

Quick example: A learner enjoys a quiz but still cannot explain why an answer is correct. Enjoyment is positive; the learning target may still need work.

Avoid: Treating satisfaction, speed, or one correct answer as sufficient evidence of learning.

2-minute background · Feedback and revision

Key idea: Useful feedback responds to an attempt and helps the learner take a productive next step; useful design feedback helps the team decide what to change.

Say to students: “A test is successful when it teaches you something about the design—even when the prototype fails.”

Quick example: If learners ignore a hint because it appears too late, revise the timing rather than adding more explanation everywhere.

Avoid: Making many changes at once so the team cannot tell what mattered.

2-minute background · Make claims the evidence can support

Key idea: One small test can show what happened in that test. It usually cannot prove that a tool works for everyone.

Say to students: “Tell the audience what you observed, what you changed, and what you still do not know.”

Quick example: Prefer “In our second test, the learner explained the graph interval without prompting” over “Our tool teaches graphing.”

Avoid: Hiding failed tests, overgeneralizing, or presenting AI-generated evaluation as learner evidence.

What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A written pre-test plan naming the tester, task, observation target, success evidence, and a signal that could require redefining the problem.
  • At least one testing record that distinguishes what the learner said from what the learner did.
  • A revision linked explicitly to evidence and, when possible, a comparable retest.
  • A sharing-event story covering the challenge, what the team learned, the design, learning-science rationale, testing, revision, demo, limitations, and next steps.
  • A focused, accessible demonstration with a backup plan.
  • A final reflection: What did this project teach me about my own learning or someone else's learning? What did I learn about using AI responsibly and effectively for learning?

Need more?

Optional extension

Test transfer or compare versions

Use a new but related task, or compare two prototype versions, to see whether the learning support carries beyond the exact example used during development.

Materials: parallel task, version A/B record, simple comparison chart.

Use when students need more support

Script the test and the story

Provide a short testing script, an observation checklist, and a presentation outline with sentence starters. Rehearse once with the advisor before the sharing event.

Materials: test-plan template, observation checklist, presentation cards, demo checklist, printed backup screenshots/materials.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Examine limitations deliberately

Ask students to design an edge-case, transfer, or accessibility test and explain how the result changes the boundary of what they can claim.

Materials: edge-case prompts, transfer task, accessibility test checklist, limitations matrix.
