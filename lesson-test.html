Your goal

Your goal

Students will plan a focused test before using the prototype, observe learner behavior and learning evidence, revise from what happened, retest when possible, and prepare to explain and demonstrate the project at the sharing event.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

Testing is where the team's design assumptions meet actual use. A learner saying “I like it” is useful feedback, but it is not the same as evidence that the target learning behavior changed.

Share is the culmination of the design process. Students should not present only a polished product; they should tell the story of the challenge, what they learned about learning and AI, what they built, what testing revealed, what changed, and what remains uncertain.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Approve who may test the prototype, what permissions are required, what may be recorded, and when testing should stop.
  • Review the team’s prototype test question and target learning behavior before the session begins.
  • Open the student Testing & Revision tool and Sharing Event Builder.
  • Prepare a comparable follow-up task for teams using the two-session version.
  • Confirm a low-tech or offline backup for the test and the eventual demonstration.

Core materials

Prototype; approved test task; Student Test and Share pages; observation notes; Testing & Revision tool; Sharing Event Builder; demonstration materials and backup.

Core activity guide

How to run each step

Open the details as you reach each activity. Explanations and student tools are placed at the moment you need them.

1Plan the test before anyone touches the prototype7–10 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Name who should test this version and why that person is appropriate.
  2. Write the exact task the tester will try.
  3. Identify 2–3 things the team will observe rather than only asking for opinions.
  4. Define what would count as evidence of progress on the learning target.
  5. Write one result that would make the team reconsider the problem definition rather than simply change the interface.
What to explain

Usability is not the same as learning

Key idea: A tool can be easy, attractive, or enjoyable without changing what a learner knows or can do. Testing should look at both usability and the target learning action.

Student-friendly language: “Ask what the learner could understand or do during or after the interaction—not only whether they liked it.”

Example: A learner may enjoy a quiz but still be unable to explain why an answer is correct.

Watch for: Do not treat satisfaction, speed, or one correct answer as sufficient learning evidence.

Finish this step with: a written test plan before testing begins.
2Run the lightest test that can answer the question10–15 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Begin with a team self-test or peer test if the prototype is still rough.
  2. Move to teacher-client or learner testing only when appropriate and approved.
  3. One student should facilitate while another observes when possible.
  4. Do not coach the tester through problems the team needs to observe.

one completed attempt that gives the team something concrete to analyze.
3Record what happened—not only what the tester said8–12 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Record where the tester begins, hesitates, asks for help, ignores something, changes strategy, or succeeds.
  2. Separate comments such as “I liked it” from behavioral evidence.
  3. Record whether the target learning behavior appeared and under what conditions.
  4. Note access or usability barriers that may have affected performance.
Open with studentsTesting & Revision · RecordCapture behavior, learning evidence, access issues, and the revision decision.
an observation record that distinguishes behavior, opinion, learning evidence, and access issues.
4Revise one high-value thing7–10 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Identify the observation most directly connected to the test question.
  2. Choose one revision that responds to that evidence.
  3. Write why the team expects the revision to help.
  4. In the 90-minute version, use a comparable task to retest. In the 45-minute version, document the planned retest if time runs out.
What to explain

Feedback and revision

Key idea: A useful test teaches the design team what to change. Changing one high-value feature at a time makes the relationship between evidence and revision clearer.

Student-friendly language: “A test is successful when it teaches you something—even when the prototype fails.”

Example: If learners ignore a hint because it arrives too late, change the timing before adding more explanation everywhere.

Watch for: Avoid changing many things at once so the team cannot tell what mattered.

Finish this step with: one evidence-linked revision and, when possible, a comparable retest.
5Turn the project into a sharing-event story10–15 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Open the Sharing Event Builder.
  2. Have students explain the challenge, what they learned from the learner and evidence, the design, why it fits learning, what testing showed, what changed, and what remains uncertain.
  3. Require a short statement about what they learned about human learning and about AI—even if the final product is non-AI.
  4. Keep claims proportional to the evidence.
What to explain

Make claims the evidence can support

Key idea: A small pilot test can show what happened in that test. It usually cannot prove that a tool works for everyone.

Student-friendly language: “Tell the audience what you observed, what you changed, and what you still do not know.”

Example: Prefer “In our second test, the learner explained the graph interval without prompting” over “Our tool teaches graphing.”

Watch for: Avoid hiding failed tests, overgeneralizing, or using AI-generated evaluation as learner evidence.

Open with studentsSharing Event BuilderBuild the evidence-based project story, limitations, and next steps.
Finish this step with: a complete project story that can be rehearsed.
6Rehearse one focused demonstration5–8 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Choose one short interaction that makes the learning design visible.
  2. Assign who introduces the problem, who demonstrates, and who explains evidence/revision.
  3. Practice the demo once without interruption.
  4. Prepare screenshots, cards, or a scripted backup in case technology fails.
  5. Ask: Can an audience member understand what the learner is supposed to think or do?

a short, understandable demo with a backup plan.
What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A written pre-test plan naming the tester, task, observation target, success evidence, and a signal that could require redefining the problem.
  • At least one testing record that distinguishes what the learner said from what the learner did.
  • A revision linked explicitly to evidence and, when possible, a comparable retest.
  • A sharing-event story covering the challenge, what the team learned, the design, learning-science rationale, testing, revision, demo, limitations, and next steps.
  • A focused, accessible demonstration with a backup plan.
  • A final reflection: What did this project teach me about my own learning or someone else's learning? What did I learn about using AI responsibly and effectively for learning?
Open the student Test stage
Need more?

Need more?

Choose only the option that responds to what you are seeing. Each adaptation has a clear purpose, a short procedure, and a stopping point so it does not turn into an extra lesson.

Optional extension

Test transfer or compare versions

Use this when: The team completed the core test and wants stronger evidence about whether the learning support extends beyond the original example.

How to run it

  1. Create a new but related task that requires the same target learning process.
  2. Predict what should transfer if the design is helping.
  3. Run the revised prototype on the new task or compare version A and B on parallel tasks.
  4. Record similarities and differences in learner behavior.
  5. Revise the team’s claim so it matches the expanded evidence.

Materials: parallel task, version A/B record, simple comparison chart.

You are ready to continue when: students can explain whether the observed improvement generalized and what remains uncertain.

Use when students need more support

Script the test and the sharing story

Use this when: Students lead the tester too much, forget what to observe, or struggle to organize the presentation.

How to run it

  1. Give students a three-part test script: introduce task, stay neutral while observing, ask two reflection questions afterward.
  2. Assign one facilitator and one observer.
  3. Use an observation checklist with boxes for hesitation, help request, successful target behavior, and access problem.
  4. After the test, complete presentation sentence starters for challenge, evidence, design, revision, and limitation.
  5. Rehearse the demonstration once with the advisor before the event.

Materials: test script, observation checklist, presentation cards, demo checklist, printed backup screenshots/materials.

You are ready to continue when: students can run a neutral test and tell the project story without relying on the advisor to explain it for them.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Test an edge case and defend the limits of the claim

Use this when: The team has a stable prototype and can reason about where it may fail.

How to run it

  1. Identify a learner, task, accessibility condition, or input that may challenge the design.
  2. Predict what the prototype will do and what would count as a failure.
  3. Run the edge-case or transfer test safely.
  4. Add the result to the limitations section of the sharing story.
  5. Explain which future test would be most valuable next.

Materials: edge-case prompts, transfer task, accessibility test checklist, limitations matrix.

You are ready to continue when: students can state not only what worked, but also the boundary of what their current evidence supports.
