Team Management, Roles, and Inclusive Participation

Help every student contribute to the learning, design, testing, and presentation work.

Teams work best when roles are clear, routines are predictable, contributions are visible, and students have multiple ways to participate. Roles may be assigned, shared, or rotated depending on the group size and setting.

Student roles

Detailed responsibilities

Peer Interviewer

Plans and conducts learner interviews. Asks about goals, existing strengths, confusion, previous strategies, and helpful or unhelpful support. Protects privacy and avoids collecting unnecessary personal information.

Key output: interview notes and evidence about the learning challenge.

Task Analyst

Breaks the STEM task into facts, concepts, procedures, decisions, reasoning, representations, and checking. Identifies where the learner's process changes or stops.

Key output: task map and precise challenge statement.

Learning-Strategy Lead

Compares evidence-based strategies and explains which one best matches the challenge. Ensures the team can describe what the learner—not only the AI—will do.

Key output: strategy rationale.

AI Interaction Designer

Defines the AI role, sequence, questions, hints, feedback, stopping points, and limits. Uses the prompt builder or a storyboard to make the interaction reviewable.

Key output: prompt or interaction flow.

Prototype Builder

Implements the agreed design using an approved platform, general AI assistant, no-code tool, or paper prototype. Documents versions rather than making unrecorded changes.

Key output: working prototype or storyboard.

Safety, Privacy, and Accessibility Reviewer

Checks data requests, accuracy rules, possible bias, age appropriateness, school policy, readability, keyboard access, and alternatives for varied learners.

Key output: completed safety and accessibility review.

User-Testing Lead

Defines the test purpose, chooses an appropriate task and participant, gives instructions, observes use, and records evidence without coaching excessively.

Key output: test plan and observation notes.

Documentation Lead

Maintains the design notebook, meeting notes, decisions, prompt versions, test evidence, revision log, AI-use disclosure, and contribution record.

Key output: complete project record.

Presentation Lead

Organizes the story of the project and helps every team member prepare to explain the learner, challenge, strategy, AI behavior, evidence, safeguards, and limitations.

Key output: presentation plan and shared speaking roles.

Group routines

A predictable structure for every meeting

  1. Opening check-in: state the meeting goal, review the previous decision, and identify barriers.
  2. Role assignment: confirm who is responsible for each task. Rotate roles when possible.
  3. Focused work block: complete one clearly defined project task.
  4. Midpoint check: ask whether the work still matches the learning goal and challenge.
  5. Peer critique: receive one strength, one question, and one evidence-based suggestion.
  6. Documentation: record decisions, contributions, versions, and unresolved issues.
  7. Closing plan: assign next steps, deadlines, and any advisor approvals needed.
Inclusive participation

Best practices for varied learners and group structures

Offer multiple roles

Students should be able to contribute through interviewing, drawing, writing, analyzing, building, testing, organizing, or presenting.

Use clear task chunks

Break complex work into visible steps with examples, checklists, and progress markers.

Rotate high-status work

Do not allow one student to control all technical decisions. Rotate building, presenting, and decision-making responsibilities.

Support different communication styles

Allow oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted forms of participation.

Design for mixed experience

Pair students strategically, separate learning-design tasks from technical tasks, and provide beginner-friendly prototype options.

Make contributions visible

Use contribution logs, short individual reflections, and team check-ins to prevent hidden or uneven participation.

Provide accessible materials

Use readable language, clear contrast, keyboard-accessible tools, captions, alternative formats, and flexible pacing.

Protect psychological safety

Critique the design rather than the person. Establish norms for respectful disagreement, uncertainty, and revision.

Reduce scope when needed

A smaller, well-tested project is better than an ambitious prototype that excludes students or cannot be completed.

Common challenges

Advisor responses

ChallengeRecommended response
One student dominates technical workRotate the builder role and require each student to explain the design rationale.
Students disagree about the projectReturn to evidence from the learner interview and compare ideas using the rubric.
Participation is unevenAssign individual deliverables, use contribution logs, and conduct brief check-ins.
A team is behindReduce the project to one learner, one challenge, one strategy, and one test case.
A student lacks technical confidenceUse paper prototypes, paired building, step-by-step models, and roles that value nontechnical expertise.
Conflict becomes personalPause the work, restate team norms, separate evidence from opinion, and use a structured decision process.
