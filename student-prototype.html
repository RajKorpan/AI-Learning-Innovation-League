What can a Learning League tool look like?

Start with the learning problem and the learner action. Technology comes later. Compare these examples before you decide what form your project should take.

Physical + AI-assisted design

Slope activity

Learner does: predict, measure, compare, and explain.

AI does: helps the team invent variations and reflection questions.

Printable + AI-assisted materials

Retrieval card challenge

Learner does: retrieve from memory, check, correct, and try again.

AI does: proposes varied questions that the team verifies.

Reusable conversational AI

Word-problem coach

Learner does: identify givens, unknowns, relationships, and a plan.

AI does: asks responsive questions without solving.

Advanced / optional

Custom digital tool

Learner does: meaningful thinking that can be observed and tested.

Team does: uses code or an advanced builder only when the extra capability is actually useful.

Three ways to build

Choose the least complicated path that fits the learning

These are paths, not achievement levels. A simple, well-tested learning experience is stronger than an impressive build that does the learner's thinking.

1

AI helps you make a learning experience

Use AI during design or material creation, but the learner-facing experience may be paper, physical, social, or non-AI.

Examples: card game, printable activity, practice routine, role-play, experiment, study guide.

2

Build a reusable AI learning tool

Create a Gem or similar conversational experience with a deliberately designed learning process.

Best when: responsive questioning, feedback, or branching conversation genuinely helps.

3

Advanced build

Use AI Studio, coding, APIs, hardware, or another application-development environment.

Optional: explore this with your advisor if your team already has the experience. It is not required for the current pilot.

A powerful hybrid pattern

AI and an off-screen activity can work together

Do→Notice→Explain→Get feedback→Try again

Example: the AI suggests a physical slope comparison → the learner tries it away from the screen → returns and explains what happened → the AI asks a focused follow-up → the learner tries another case.

Teach the AI how to support learning Guided Prompt Builder
Why so many questions?
Every answer changes the design of the AI learning tool
Learning goal+Learner+Breakdown+Learning strategy+Feedback+Activity=Designed learning interaction
Basic prompt
“Help me understand slope.”
Designed prompt
“Act as a reflection coach for a learner who can calculate slope but has difficulty connecting rise/run to rate of change. Ask for a prediction and explanation before giving a hint…”
Every answer you give changes what the AI is supposed to do, what the learner must do, and what counts as useful feedback.
This is not a shortcut around prompt writing. You write a first attempt, then the builder asks you to make your learning-design decisions explicit. Afterward you compare, test, and revise.
Your prompt-learning cycle
First prompt→Draft 1→Test 1→Draft 2→Test again
What should your first prompt do?
Explain the AI’s job in plain language: what it should help the learner do, how it should interact, and what it should avoid doing. Do not try to make it perfect. The guided builder will help you notice what is missing.

Required build checkpoint
Before polishing, confirm the learning goal, learner thinking, learning strategy, build path, meaningful role of AI (if any), accessibility, privacy/platform requirements, and the first test question. A simpler path is always acceptable when it better fits the learning.


Accessibility check
Can the learner understand the instructions, control the pace, and access the important information? Does the design rely heavily on reading, sound, vision, precise motor input, one device, or reliable internet? Build an alternative where needed.
