Step 3

See what projects can look like

See possibilities before choosing

Learning tools can look very different

Start with what the learner needs to do. Then choose the simplest way to practice it. AI can help design the activity without being the whole activity.

📏
Mostly off-screen

Physical slope challenge

Learner: predicts, measures, compares, and explains.

AI: helps the team invent variations and reflection questions.

🃏
Printable / game

Retrieval card challenge

Learner: recalls from memory, checks, corrects, and tries again.

AI: proposes varied questions that the team verifies before using.

💬
Interactive AI

Word-problem coach

Learner: identifies givens, unknowns, relationships, and a plan.

AI: asks responsive questions without solving the problem.

🧩
Hybrid

Activity + AI reflection

Learner: completes a physical activity, notices what happened, and explains it.

AI: asks a follow-up, gives feedback, or suggests the next challenge.

🛠️
Go further

Custom digital tool

Learner: still does meaningful thinking that can be observed and tested.

Team: uses coding or an advanced builder only when the extra capability improves the learning experience.

Small is good.A small tool that helps with one real learning problem is better than a complicated tool that tries to solve everything.

Why am I answering these questions?

You are designing the behavior of the learning tool


Each answer controls a different part of the experience. The builder is not trying to make the prompt longer—it is helping you decide what the learner and the AI should actually do.

Vague prompt
“Help me understand slope.”
Likely result: a generic explanation or answer.
→
Designed prompt
“Help a learner connect rise/run to rate of change…”
Likely result: a targeted interaction that asks the learner to predict, explain, receive a hint, and try again.


Every answer you give changes what the AI is supposed to do, what the learner must do, or what counts as useful feedback.


This is not a shortcut around prompt writing. You write a first attempt, then the builder asks you to make your learning-design decisions explicit. Afterward you compare, test, and revise.
How you will learn prompting
1. Try it yourself→2. See what happens→3. Try a strong example→4. Build your own→5. Compare→6. Test→7. Change something→8. Test again
Your prompt-learning cycle
First prompt→Draft 1→Test 1→Draft 2→Test again
What should your first prompt do?
Explain the AI’s job in plain language: what it should help the learner do, how it should interact, and what it should avoid doing. Do not try to make it perfect. The guided builder will help you notice what is missing.
