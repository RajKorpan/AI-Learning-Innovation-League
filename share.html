Share

Present the learning challenge, design reasoning, evidence, and limitations clearly.

A strong presentation is not a sales pitch for a chatbot. It is a concise, evidence-based explanation of what the team learned and how the design changed.

Presentation structure

A clear five- to seven-minute project story

  1. Introduce the learner and goal: describe the intended learner without revealing private information.
  2. Explain the challenge: show the evidence and task analysis that led to the diagnosis.
  3. Justify the strategy: explain why the selected learning strategy fits the challenge.
  4. Demonstrate the interaction: show a short, realistic example of the learner using the tool.
  5. Share evidence: describe what happened during testing and what the team observed.
  6. Explain revisions: compare versions and connect each change to evidence.
  7. State safeguards and limits: explain privacy, accuracy, accessibility, and when the tool should not be used.
Best practices

Make the presentation understandable and credible

Lead with the learning problem

Explain the learner's need before discussing the platform or technical features.

Use one strong example

A short interaction is more useful than a long live demonstration that may fail or distract.

Show evidence visually

Use a task map, prompt comparison, observation excerpt, or version timeline.

Share speaking roles

Every team member should contribute and be ready to answer questions.

Describe limitations honestly

Credibility increases when students can explain errors, constraints, and next steps.

Protect privacy

Remove names, personal details, account information, and sensitive examples from slides and demonstrations.

Design accessible slides

Use large text, high contrast, descriptive headings, limited text, captions, and verbal descriptions of visuals.

Rehearse transitions

Practice who speaks, when the demo begins, and how the team responds if technology fails.

Prepare for questions

Expect questions about evidence, strategy, bias, privacy, accessibility, testing, and student ownership.

Recommended slide sequence

Eight slides are usually enough

SlideContent
1Project title, team, intended learner, and learning goal
2Specific learning challenge and evidence
3Task analysis
4Learning strategy and rationale
5AI interaction or prototype
6User-testing evidence
7Revisions, safeguards, and accessibility
8Limitations, lessons learned, and next steps

Presentation checklist

  • Every claim is understandable to someone outside the team.
  • The demonstration uses non-sensitive information.
  • Students explain why the strategy fits the challenge.
  • Testing evidence is specific rather than only positive opinion.
  • Revisions are connected to evidence.
  • Limitations are stated clearly.
  • Slides are readable and accessible.
  • A backup plan exists if the technology fails.
