Follow one implementation sequence

These steps combine readiness, club setup, the advisor cycle, and the full implementation sequence. Each step links to the detailed guidance on this page.

  1. Build a starter club plan and confirm readiness, permissions, technology, and access.
  2. Choose the implementation format.
  3. Recruit students and connect with teacher-clients and families.
  4. Form the team, establish rotating roles, and choose whether each project starts from the student's own learning or someone else's.
  5. Select a schedule for the guided experience.
  6. Guide the five lessons through the six-stage student journey: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Share.
  7. Maintain advisor boundaries and student ownership.
  8. Integrate safety, accessibility, responsible AI, and family communication.
  9. Approve external testing and final sharing-event preparation.

Build your club plan

Use this adaptive planner to generate recommendations for team structure, meeting design, technology, teacher-client work, accessibility, family communication, the sharing event, and an adjusted calendar. Review the plan with your school and participants before implementation.

How the planner makes recommendations

The planner is a transparent rule-based tool. It does not use generative AI, send information to a server, or save your entries.

Every selection affects at least one part of the plan. Combination rules identify conflicts—for example, a virtual club without devices, advanced application development without technical support, or a compressed five-week schedule with short meetings and students who are new to design.

Recommendations are starting points rather than automatic decisions. Advisors should review them with students, families, teacher-clients, school leaders, and accessibility staff as appropriate.

1. Confirm readiness

People and permissions

  • Advisor or advisor team
  • Student team
  • Teacher-client
  • Peer learners or reviewers
  • Family communication
  • Required school permissions

Technology and space

  • Meeting space or virtual platform
  • Devices and internet access
  • Approved AI tools
  • Paper and low-tech alternatives
  • Technical support and backups

Safety and accessibility

  • Privacy and data minimization
  • Age and account requirements
  • Reading, visual, hearing, motor, language, and cognitive access
  • Stopping and escalation procedures
Start small: One advisor, one teacher-client, five students, one shared product, one low-resolution prototype, and one local sharing event are enough for a complete first cycle.

Complete before students begin

  • Advisor and schedule confirmed
  • School and platform requirements reviewed
  • Family communication prepared
  • Teacher-client recruitment started
  • Accessible meeting and technology options identified

Complete during the first meeting

  • Explain the League and one shared team product
  • Establish team agreements and rotating roles
  • Review AI literacy, privacy, and accessibility expectations
  • Introduce the design cycle and project notebook
  • Schedule interview practice and next steps
Every team studies AI literacy. Students examine human-versus-machine reasoning, prompting, output evaluation, privacy, bias, and responsible use even when the final learning tool is non-AI.

2. Choose a format

One-on-one tutoring

A teacher guides one student through the lessons and design process. Add teacher-client, family, peer, or virtual feedback so the student designs for another learner.

Small-group team

Students share one product, rotate roles, interview one another, critique across teams, and test with peers.

Classroom integration

Teach the five lessons in class and use class, independent, or club time for project work.

Virtual club

Use shared documents, breakout groups, clear agendas, accessible tools, and privacy-safe examples.

Lunch or short-cycle club

Use shorter mini-lessons and reduce scope to one component, one strategy, and one prototype question.

Boot camp or showcase day

Use pre-work and stations for empathy synthesis, analysis, prototyping, testing, revision, and presentation.

3. Recruit students and connect with teacher-clients and families

Teacher-client connection

  • Identify a teacher who can describe a difficult-to-teach concept or a prerequisite students often lack.
  • Schedule a practice interview before the real conversation.
  • Ask for a recent example, signs of confusion, current supports, and what successful performance looks like.
  • Keep the first conversation focused on understanding rather than proposing solutions.

Family communication

  • Explain the purpose of the League and the shared team product.
  • Explain how AI may and may not be used.
  • Describe interviews, testing, sharing, privacy, accommodations, and how families can provide feedback.
  • Provide confirmed event and logistics information when available.

4. Form and facilitate the team

Five-student role model

RoleResponsibility
Interview and Empathy LeadPrepares questions, follows stories, protects permission and privacy, and synthesizes needs.
Learning Research LeadCoordinates task analysis, source verification, evidence strength, learning myths, and progression mapping.
Design and Prototype LeadOrganizes ideation, product comparison, prototypes, and version documentation.
Testing and Evidence LeadDefines test questions, stages tests, separates statements from behavior, and links evidence to revision.
Documentation and Presentation LeadMaintains the design notebook, contributions, educational justification, and accessible sharing-event presentation.

Roles should rotate or overlap. Every student should understand the full project.

Meeting routine

  1. Review the current design mode and evidence needed.
  2. Assign or rotate roles.
  3. Complete one focused task.
  4. Pause for learning-goal, safety, and accessibility checks.
  5. Receive one strength, one question, and one evidence-based critique.
  6. Record decisions, assumptions, versions, and contributions.
  7. Identify the next action and whether the team must move backward.

Inclusive participation

  • Offer oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted ways to contribute.
  • Separate technical building from educational decision-making.
  • Rotate high-status tasks such as building and presenting.
  • Use clear task chunks, models, checklists, and flexible pacing.
  • Reduce scope instead of excluding a student.
  • Critique the design, not the person.

Facilitator view: six stages, five lessons

The student experience uses six visible design stages. The five-lesson curriculum fits underneath that journey: Lesson 1 = Empathize, Lesson 2 = Define, Lesson 3 = Ideate, Lesson 4 = Prototype, and Lesson 5 = Test + Share. A stage can take more than one meeting. Use the core path first; optional extensions are there when students need more background, support, or challenge.

1EmpathizeUnderstand the challenge2DefineFind the breakdown3IdeateInvent possibilities4PrototypeBuild something small5TestPut it to the test6ShareTell the story
Two outcomes matter throughout.

Students design something that may help themselves or someone else learn, while also developing their own understanding of human learning and AI. Ask about both kinds of learning at every checkpoint.

7. Advisor role and boundaries

Advisors should

  • Facilitate the curriculum and design process.
  • Help students access teacher-clients and credible sources.
  • Ask for evidence and reasoning.
  • Approve tools, safety, accessibility, testing, and sharing.
  • Support conflict resolution and inclusive participation.
  • Reduce scope when needed.
  • Keep student ownership visible.

Advisors should not

  • Select the solution for students.
  • Rewrite the student product.
  • Lead interviews in place of students.
  • Overvalue technical complexity.
  • Allow AI to make unsupported educational decisions.
  • Permit identifiable data or unapproved tools.
  • Coach so much that testing no longer reflects the product.

8. Review safety, accessibility, and family communication

Advisors are responsible for making these checks concrete. Do not ask students only whether a tool is “accessible” or “safe.” Use the questions below to review the activity, prototype, platform, testing plan, and presentation.

Stage-by-stage advisor responsibilities

StageWhat the advisor should verify
ResearchStudents verify claims against original or credible sources, distinguish evidence from suggestion, document uncertainty, and avoid private learner information.
DesignThe team minimizes data, examines bias, preserves learner agency, avoids answer substitution, considers multiple representations, and compares simpler alternatives.
BuildingThe tool uses age-appropriate language, states its limits, includes accessible interaction options, has a low-tech alternative, and has been tested with edge cases.
TestingThe participant, task, permissions, stopping rule, evidence target, realistic context, and response to frustration or unexpected output are approved in advance.
PresentationIdentifying details are removed, permissions are confirmed, limitations are explained honestly, and the presentation and demonstration have accessible alternatives.

Accessibility questions for advisors

Reading and language

  • Can students and intended learners understand the directions, questions, feedback, and vocabulary?
  • Are key terms defined in context?
  • Are shorter sentences, plain-language directions, translation, or language supports needed?
  • Can the same information be communicated orally or through another format?

Visual access

  • Is text large enough and is contrast strong enough?
  • Is meaning available without relying on color alone?
  • Do diagrams, graphs, icons, and images have labels or text descriptions?
  • Can the learner zoom or use a screen reader?

Hearing access

  • Are captions or transcripts available for audio and video?
  • Are spoken directions also available in writing?
  • Can alerts, feedback, and timing cues be perceived without sound?

Motor and interaction access

  • Can the tool be used without precise dragging, rapid clicking, or timed responses?
  • Can it be operated with a keyboard, touch, switch, speech, or another input method?
  • Are buttons and targets large enough and clearly labeled?

Cognitive load and attention

  • Are instructions, choices, and screens manageable without hiding the overall goal?
  • Can steps be chunked, revisited, or paused?
  • Are unnecessary animations, distractions, or competing information removed?
  • Does feedback clearly identify the next useful action?

Participation and expression

  • Can students contribute orally, in writing, visually, asynchronously, or with assistance?
  • Can learners demonstrate understanding in more than one appropriate way?
  • Are technical tasks separated from educational decision-making so all students can contribute?

Technology and device access

  • Does the tool work on the devices and internet connections participants actually have?
  • Does it require an account, paid feature, installation, or permission that creates a barrier?
  • Can work be saved, printed, or continued when connectivity fails?

Low-tech alternative

  • Can the same learning goal be supported with paper, cards, role-play, a checklist, or another non-digital format?
  • Can the team test the educational interaction before depending on the platform?
  • Is there a backup for the final demonstration?

Questions to ask during testing

  • Who is unable to begin or continue without extra explanation?
  • Which directions, representations, controls, or feedback create confusion?
  • Does the tool increase frustration or cognitive load without supporting learning?
  • Does the learner have a meaningful way to pause, stop, ask for help, or use an alternative?
  • Did the team test with people whose access needs differ from the designers’ own needs?

Family communication for advisors

  • Explain what students will do and that the team may create an AI or non-AI learning tool.
  • Explain what information students must not enter into AI tools.
  • Explain interviews, notes or recordings, prototype testing, public sharing, and media permissions.
  • Tell families how to request an accommodation or alternative participation format.
  • Share confirmed sharing-event logistics, including location, virtual access, transportation, food, interpretation, accessibility, and media expectations.
  • Direct questions to the program contact listed on the Resources page.

9. Approve testing, revision, and presentation

  1. Team self-test
  2. Peer-team test
  3. Teacher-client review
  4. Learner test when appropriate and approved
  5. Revised test using a comparable task

Require teams to record

  • What the user tried to do
  • Where the user hesitated or misunderstood
  • What the user ignored or used successfully
  • What the user said and what the user actually did
  • Whether the target learning behavior changed
  • What revision follows from the evidence

Sharing-event presentations should explain the teacher-client need, empathy findings, smallest component, learning-science evidence, product choice, prototype, testing, revisions, responsible AI, accessibility, impact, limitations, and team reflection.
