Follow one implementation sequence

These steps combine readiness, club setup, the advisor cycle, and the full implementation sequence. Each step links to the detailed guidance on this page.

  1. Confirm readiness, people, permissions, technology, and access.
  2. Choose the implementation format.
  3. Recruit students and connect with teacher-clients and families.
  4. Form the team, assign rotating roles, and establish routines.
  5. Select a schedule and guide the full League cycle.
  6. Teach the five-lesson curriculum while students create project artifacts.
  7. Maintain advisor boundaries and student ownership.
  8. Review responsible AI, privacy, accessibility, and family communication.
  9. Approve testing, evidence collection, revision, and final presentation.
  10. Reflect on implementation and begin the next design cycle.

1. Confirm readiness

People and permissions

  • Advisor or advisor team
  • Student team
  • Teacher-client
  • Peer learners or reviewers
  • Family communication
  • Required school permissions

Technology and space

  • Meeting space or virtual platform
  • Devices and internet access
  • Approved AI tools
  • Paper and low-tech alternatives
  • Technical support and backups

Safety and accessibility

  • Privacy and data minimization
  • Age and account requirements
  • Reading, visual, hearing, motor, language, and cognitive access
  • Stopping and escalation procedures
Start small: One advisor, one teacher-client, five students, one shared product, one low-resolution prototype, and one local presentation are enough for a complete first cycle.

2. Choose a format

One-on-one tutoring

A teacher guides one student through the lessons and design process. Add teacher-client, family, peer, or virtual feedback so the student designs for another learner.

Small-group team

Students share one product, rotate roles, interview one another, critique across teams, and test with peers.

Classroom integration

Teach the five lessons in class and use class, independent, or club time for project work.

Virtual club

Use shared documents, breakout groups, clear agendas, accessible tools, and privacy-safe examples.

Lunch or short-cycle club

Use shorter mini-lessons and reduce scope to one component, one strategy, and one prototype question.

Boot camp or showcase day

Use pre-work and stations for empathy synthesis, analysis, prototyping, testing, revision, and presentation.

3. Recruit students and connect with teacher-clients and families

Teacher-client connection

  • Identify a teacher who can describe a difficult-to-teach concept or a prerequisite students often lack.
  • Schedule a practice interview before the real conversation.
  • Ask for a recent example, signs of confusion, current supports, and what successful performance looks like.
  • Keep the first conversation focused on understanding rather than proposing solutions.

Family communication

  • Explain the purpose of the League and the shared team product.
  • Explain how AI may and may not be used.
  • Describe interviews, testing, sharing, privacy, accommodations, and how families can provide feedback.
  • Provide confirmed event and logistics information when available.

4. Form and facilitate the team

Five-student role model

RoleResponsibility
Interview and Empathy LeadPrepares questions, follows stories, protects permission and privacy, and synthesizes needs.
Learning Research LeadCoordinates task analysis, source verification, evidence strength, learning myths, and progression mapping.
Design and Prototype LeadOrganizes ideation, product comparison, prototypes, and version documentation.
Testing and Evidence LeadDefines test questions, stages tests, separates statements from behavior, and links evidence to revision.
Documentation and Presentation LeadMaintains the design notebook, contributions, educational justification, and accessible presentation.

Roles should rotate or overlap. Every student should understand the full project.

Meeting routine

  1. Review the current design mode and evidence needed.
  2. Assign or rotate roles.
  3. Complete one focused task.
  4. Pause for learning-goal, safety, and accessibility checks.
  5. Receive one strength, one question, and one evidence-based critique.
  6. Record decisions, assumptions, versions, and contributions.
  7. Identify the next action and whether the team must move backward.

Inclusive participation

  • Offer oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted ways to contribute.
  • Separate technical building from educational decision-making.
  • Rotate high-status tasks such as building and presenting.
  • Use clear task chunks, models, checklists, and flexible pacing.
  • Reduce scope instead of excluding a student.
  • Critique the design, not the person.

6. Five-lesson curriculum

The five lessons provide a common foundation and produce the artifacts needed for the team project.

Lesson 1: Understand Human Learning and AI

Essential question

How can a tool support learning without replacing the learner’s thinking?

Students will

  • Explain why obtaining an answer is not the same as learning.
  • Describe productive struggle, metacognition, self-regulation, prior knowledge, feedback, cognitive load, and transfer.
  • Identify AI as a research partner, design assistant, building platform, delivery mechanism, and critical object of study.
  • Explain when a non-AI tool may be better.
  • Reject fixed learning-style labels as a design basis.

Suggested activities

  1. Compare a learner solving a problem with an AI generating a response.
  2. Sort examples into learning support, answer substitution, research, design, delivery, and critical study.
  3. Rewrite a learning myth as an evidence-based design question.
  4. Create team principles for AI that supports learning.

Project artifact: Human-learning and AI design principles.

Lesson 2: Listen Through Empathy Interviews

Essential question

What can students learn by listening before designing?

  • Explain why empathy comes before solution design.
  • Use starter questions flexibly and ask follow-ups.
  • Listen for stories, emotions, needs, strategies, barriers, and workarounds.
  • Ask permission and protect privacy.
  • Synthesize findings without leading the interviewee.

Suggested activities

  1. Compare leading and non-leading questions.
  2. Practice with a teammate or chatbot role-play.
  3. Conduct a teacher-client interview.
  4. Conduct or arrange a peer learner interview.
  5. Create insight statements and list changed assumptions.

Project artifact: Empathy notes and synthesized insights.

Lesson 3: Define and Research the Problem

Essential question

What is the smallest meaningful component, and what evidence should guide the design?

  • Use the Problem Size Check.
  • Break a task into knowledge, actions, prerequisites, vocabulary, concepts, procedures, decisions, errors, and cognitive-load points.
  • Select one likely bottleneck.
  • Evaluate evidence strength and identify learning myths.
  • Map beginning, developing, and proficient performance.

Suggested activities

  1. Revise a broad problem into a precise statement.
  2. Complete the Task Analysis tool.
  3. Add at least three verified evidence records.
  4. Create a learning progression.
  5. Use the Strategy Matcher as a starting point, not a diagnosis.

Project artifact: Defined problem, task map, evidence record, and progression.

Lesson 4: Ideate and Prototype

Essential question

What is the simplest learning tool that can test the team’s main assumption?

  • Generate at least three meaningfully different concepts.
  • Compare AI and non-AI options.
  • Explain whether AI adds genuine value.
  • Select a product type aligned with the desired learner action.
  • Create a low-resolution prototype.
  • Apply privacy, agency, accessibility, and fading checks.

Suggested activities

  1. Brainstorm paper, activity, AI-assisted, conversational, and custom options.
  2. Use a product decision guide.
  3. Create a paper flow, storyboard, role-play, prompt skeleton, sample set, or wireframe.
  4. Use the Prompt Builder when conversational AI fits.
  5. Write the prototype question and test plan.

Project artifact: Concept comparison, product decision, and low-resolution prototype.

Lesson 5: Test, Improve, Reflect, and Share

Essential question

What happened when people used the tool, and what should the team change?

  • Use staged testing and realistic tasks.
  • Distinguish what users say from what users do.
  • Record target learning behavior and unexpected outcomes.
  • Revise from evidence and consider redefining the problem.
  • Complete reflection and an educational justification.

Suggested activities

  1. Conduct a team self-test and peer-team test.
  2. Prepare teacher-client and learner testing.
  3. Record hesitation, misunderstanding, successful use, ignored features, statements, actions, and learning evidence.
  4. Revise and retest.
  5. Prepare an accessible presentation.

Project artifact: Testing record, revision log, educational justification, and presentation.

7. Advisor role and boundaries

Advisors should

  • Facilitate the curriculum and design process.
  • Help students access teacher-clients and credible sources.
  • Ask for evidence and reasoning.
  • Approve tools, safety, accessibility, testing, and sharing.
  • Support conflict resolution and inclusive participation.
  • Reduce scope when needed.
  • Keep student ownership visible.

Advisors should not

  • Select the solution for students.
  • Rewrite the student product.
  • Lead interviews in place of students.
  • Overvalue technical complexity.
  • Allow AI to make unsupported educational decisions.
  • Permit identifiable data or unapproved tools.
  • Coach so much that testing no longer reflects the product.

8. Advisor safety, accessibility, and family communication checklist

During researchRequire source verification, original-source checks, uncertainty, and no private learner information.
During designMinimize data, examine bias, preserve agency, avoid answer substitution, and compare simpler alternatives.
During buildingUse age-appropriate language, define limits, include accessible interactions, and test edge cases.
During testingApprove the participant, task, permission, stopping rule, evidence target, and realistic but safe context.
During presentationRemove identifying details, confirm permissions, explain limitations, and provide accessible materials.

  • Explain what students will do and how AI will and will not be used.
  • Explain what information students must not enter into AI tools.
  • Explain interviews, recordings, testing, sharing, and media permissions.
  • Describe accommodations and how families can request support.
  • Share event logistics when confirmed.

9. Approve testing, revision, and presentation

  1. Team self-test
  2. Peer-team test
  3. Teacher-client review
  4. Learner test when appropriate and approved
  5. Revised test using a comparable task

Require teams to record

  • What the user tried to do
  • Where the user hesitated or misunderstood
  • What the user ignored or used successfully
  • What the user said and what the user actually did
  • Whether the target learning behavior changed
  • What revision follows from the evidence

Final presentations should explain the teacher-client need, empathy findings, smallest component, learning-science evidence, product choice, prototype, testing, revisions, responsible AI, accessibility, impact, limitations, and team reflection.

10. Reflect and begin the next cycle

  • What parts of the schedule were realistic?
  • Where did students need more modeling or structure?
  • Did the advisor preserve student ownership?
  • Which privacy, technology, or accessibility barriers appeared?
  • What did teacher-clients, learners, and families contribute?
  • What should be revised before the next cycle?
