What students gain—and how you support them

The League should feel more like a design, robotics, or debate club than another class. Students build useful skills because they need them to solve a real learning problem—not because they are completing a sequence of lessons.

What students gain

  • Understand a real learner and learning challenge.
  • Practice interviewing, observation, and evidence-based decisions.
  • Apply useful ideas from learning science.
  • Build AI literacy by deciding when AI genuinely adds value.
  • Prototype and test ideas instead of waiting for a perfect solution.
  • Explain what they learned, built, tested, and changed.

Guide the thinking, not the answer

Do

  • Ask questions that help students explain their thinking.
  • Help narrow projects that become too broad.
  • Keep attention on the learner and learning problem.
  • Help students notice what evidence is telling them.
  • Adjust support based on the team's experience.

Avoid

  • Choosing the solution.
  • Conducting interviews for students.
  • Building the prototype for them.
  • Giving an answer when a useful question could move the thinking forward.
  • Assuming a more technical solution is automatically stronger.

You do not need to be an AI or learning-science expert. Each session gives you the explanations, examples, activities, and facilitation guidance you need at that point.

Start facilitating the League

Facilitate the League

Five guided sessions take students from understanding a learner to testing and sharing a solution. Use the sequence as a scaffold rather than a rigid course: provide more support when the process is new and step back as students become more confident.

Before each session

  • Try the student activity yourself.
  • Review the facilitator explanation and example.
  • Decide whether your students need any adaptation or additional support.

During the session: Let students make decisions first and step in when they are stuck, losing focus, or need access or safety support.
