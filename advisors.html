Start here

  1. Confirm an advisor, student team, teacher-client, schedule, space, technology options, and permissions.
  2. Explain that students will create one shared learning tool that may or may not use AI.
  3. Communicate with families about participation, privacy, accessibility, testing, and events.
  4. Choose a format and schedule.
  5. Teach the five-lesson curriculum while students complete project artifacts.

Advisor role and boundaries

Advisors should

  • Teach or coordinate the curriculum.
  • Help students access teacher-clients and credible research.
  • Ask for evidence and reasoning.
  • Approve tools, safety, accessibility, testing, and sharing.
  • Support conflict resolution and inclusive participation.
  • Reduce scope when needed.
  • Document implementation and participant feedback.

Advisors should not

  • Select the solution.
  • Rewrite the student product.
  • Lead interviews in place of students.
  • Overvalue technical complexity.
  • Allow AI to make unsupported educational decisions.
  • Treat the showcase as the only goal.
  • Overstate impact from a small project.

Five simplified team roles

RolePrimary responsibility
Interview and Empathy LeadConversations, follow-up questions, synthesis, permission, and privacy

Design and Prototype LeadMultiple concepts, product type, prototype, and version record

Roles should rotate or overlap. Every student should understand the entire product. Offer oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted ways to participate.

Safety, accessibility, and family communication

  • Review age, account, platform, school, privacy, and sharing requirements.
  • Use non-identifying examples and minimize data.
  • Provide low-tech alternatives.
  • Consider reading, visual, hearing, motor, language, cognitive-load, and device access.
  • Explain what students will do and how AI will and will not be used.
  • Explain testing, events, permissions, accommodations, food, transportation, and other logistics when confirmed.
  • Provide a contact for concerns.

Use the rubric and prepare for a showcase

Students should present empathy findings, the defined problem, task analysis, learning-science evidence, product-type decision, prototype, testing, revisions, accessibility, responsible AI, impact, limitations, and team reflection.

Advisor cycle

  1. Confirm readiness, permissions, format, schedule, and one team product.
  2. Connect students with a teacher-client and practice empathy interviews.
  3. Teach the five-lesson curriculum while students produce project artifacts.
  4. Review problem size, task analysis, evidence, and product-type decision.
  5. Approve the prototype, AI use, accessibility, and testing plan.
  6. Facilitate testing, evidence-based revision, reflection, and team conflict resolution.
  7. Use the rubric formatively and prepare students for a showcase level.
  8. Complete required evaluation responsibilities and document implementation lessons.

What advisors should not do

  • Select the solution for students.
  • Rewrite the student product.
  • Lead empathy interviews in place of students.
  • Overvalue technical complexity.
  • Allow AI to make unsupported educational decisions.
  • Treat the showcase as the only goal.
  • Permit identifiable data or unapproved tools.
  • Claim impact that the evidence does not support.

Troubleshooting

ProblemAdvisor response
Students jump to a chatbot idea.Return to empathy, problem size, and the “Does AI add value?” decision guide.
The teacher-client problem is too broad.Use task analysis to select one prerequisite, decision, representation, or mental action.
The team disagrees.Compare options using evidence, constraints, and the rubric rather than status or preference.
A student dominates the build.Rotate roles and require every student to explain the educational justification.
Testing produces weak evidence.Clarify the target behavior, use a realistic task, and compare what the learner said with what the learner did.
Technology fails.Use the paper, storyboard, role-play, or screenshot fallback.
Start and run a League team

Ten-part implementation sequence

  1. Before you begin: confirm purpose, advisor, permissions, technology, safety, accessibility, space, and time.
  2. Choose a format: one-on-one, small group, classroom, virtual, lunch, boot camp, or semester.
  3. Recruit students: explain the shared team product, time, interviews, testing, reflection, and showcase.
  4. Connect with teachers and families: recruit teacher-clients and communicate privacy, logistics, accommodations, and feedback roles.
  5. Form the team: assign and rotate five simplified roles.
  6. Select a schedule: five-week cycle, six-week, ten-week, or semester.
  7. Follow the design cycle: empathize, define, research, ideate, prototype, and test iteratively.
  8. Test projects: self-test, peer test, teacher-client review, learner test, and revised test.
  9. Prepare a showcase: select the appropriate competition level and product track.
  10. Reflect and begin another cycle: decide whether to redefine, redesign, extend, or stop.

Advisor preparation

Confirm people, permissions, teacher-client, team, technology options, family communication, safety, accessibility, evaluation, and showcase format.

Week 1

Understand and Listen — Empathize

Human learning and AI mini-lesson; practice interview; teacher-client interview; peer learner interview; synthesis.

Outputs: empathy notes, changed assumptions, candidate needs.

Week 2

Diagnose and Research — Define

Problem Size Check; task analysis; smallest meaningful component; learning progression; evidence record.

Outputs: precise problem, task map, evidence claims, design implications.

Week 3

Imagine and Prototype — Ideate + Prototype

Generate at least three product types; decide whether AI adds value; create a low-resolution prototype.

Outputs: concept comparison, prototype, test question, safety checkpoint.

Week 4

Test and Improve — Test

Team self-test, peer-team test, teacher-client review, learner test when approved, revision, and revised test.

Outputs: observation record, changed assumptions, revision log, version comparison.

Week 5

Share and Reflect

Complete rubric, educational justification, presentation, team reflection, family/community feedback, and next-cycle decision.

Outputs: presentation, final prototype, evidence summary, limitations, reflection.

Team facilitation and inclusive participation

Five team roles

Interview and Empathy Lead

Prepares starter questions, follows stories, protects permission and privacy, synthesizes needs, and checks that design remains grounded in people.

Learning Research Lead

Coordinates task analysis, source verification, evidence strength, learning myths, progression mapping, and design implications.

Design and Prototype Lead

Organizes ideation, compares product types, creates low-resolution prototypes, and records versions without controlling all technical work.

Testing and Evidence Lead

Defines test questions, stages tests, separates user statements from behavior, and connects observations to revisions.

Documentation and Presentation Lead

Maintains the design notebook, contribution log, rubric evidence, educational justification, and accessible presentation.

Group routine

  1. Review the current design mode and the evidence needed.
  2. Assign or rotate roles.
  3. Complete one focused task.
  4. Pause for a safety, accessibility, and learning-goal check.
  5. Receive one strength, one question, and one evidence-based critique.
  6. Record decisions, assumptions, versions, and contributions.
  7. Identify the next action and whether the team must move backward.

Inclusive participation

  • Offer oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted ways to contribute.
  • Separate technical building from educational decision-making.
  • Use clear task chunks, models, checklists, and flexible pacing.
  • Rotate high-status tasks such as building and presenting.
  • Use accessible tools and low-tech alternatives.
  • Reduce scope instead of excluding a team member.
  • Critique the design, not the person.
  • Record individual and shared contributions.
Responsible AI, safety, privacy, and accessibility

Stage-by-stage checks

StageRequired checks
ResearchVerify sources; check hallucinations; distinguish evidence from suggestion; record uncertainty.
DesignProtect privacy; minimize data; consider bias; preserve learner agency; avoid answer substitution.
BuildUse age-appropriate language; state limits; include accessible interaction and alternatives; test edge cases.
TestUse non-identifying data; allow users to stop; observe harm, frustration, or exclusion; document unexpected outputs.
ShareRemove identifying details; obtain permissions; explain limitations honestly; provide access accommodations.

Accessibility prompts

Reading level

Can the intended learner understand the instructions and feedback?

Visual access

Is contrast readable? Is meaning available without color alone? Are visuals described?

Hearing access

Are captions, transcripts, or text alternatives available?

Motor access

Can the tool be used with keyboard, switch, touch, or alternative input?

Language access

Are key terms defined? Are translations or language supports needed?

Cognitive load

Are steps, choices, and displays manageable without hiding the whole task?

Device availability

Can the tool work on available devices and connections?

Low-tech alternative

Can the same learning goal be reached without the platform?

Stop and involve an advisor when

  • A user shares sensitive personal information.
  • The tool generates harmful, discriminatory, or age-inappropriate output.
  • The project makes medical, psychological, disability, placement, or other high-stakes claims.
  • The platform or sharing setting is not approved.
  • A user becomes distressed or wants to stop.
  • Students cannot explain or verify an important claim.
Participant feedback and evaluation responsibilities

Evidence sources

Student artifacts

Empathy notes, problem definitions, task maps, evidence records, prototypes, testing logs, revisions, and presentations.

Student feedback

Experience, confidence, understanding, collaboration, responsible AI, and perceived value.

Teacher and advisor feedback

Clarity, feasibility, time, technology, safety, accessibility, student engagement, and revisions.

Family feedback

Transparency, privacy, support, accommodations, value, event experience, and trust.

Usability evidence

Whether users can find and apply the website guidance without excessive explanation.

Implementation record

Attendance, timing, deviations, barriers, supports, and contextual conditions.

Advisor responsibilities

  • Use the program-provided feedback forms and instructions.
  • Explain which activities are part of the League and which feedback activities are optional.
  • Follow required permissions, privacy, and data-handling rules.
  • Use the program’s approved explanation of privacy, participation, and possible benefits.
  • Record implementation changes and barriers.
  • Report concerns to the project team.
  • Do not upload identifiable participant feedback to AI tools.

Interpret small-cycle findings carefully

A small implementation cycle can show usability, completion, patterns, and promising evidence. It should not be described as proving broad impact or effectiveness.

Competition-level event

Teams present at Prospective Prototyping, Product Showcase, or Educational Justification level.

Community co-design at the event

  • Teacher-clients review whether the product addresses the original need.
  • Learners describe clarity, usefulness, frustration, and access.
  • Families provide feedback on transparency, privacy, accommodations, and value.
  • Peers identify evidence, assumptions, and revision opportunities.
  • Judges reward educational quality—not technical complexity alone.
