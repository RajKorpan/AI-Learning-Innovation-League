Teachers and advisors

Start and guide a League team

This page is a complete advisor guide: readiness, formats, schedules, teacher-client connections, family communication, team facilitation, safety, curriculum, testing, showcase preparation, and feedback.

Start here

  1. Confirm an advisor, student team, teacher-client, schedule, space, technology options, and permissions.
  2. Explain that students will create one shared learning tool that may or may not use AI.
  3. Communicate with families about participation, privacy, accessibility, testing, and events.
  4. Choose a format and schedule.
  5. Teach the five-lesson curriculum while students complete project artifacts.
  6. Approve the problem definition, evidence, prototype, safety plan, and testing plan.
  7. Use feedback and the rubric formatively.
  8. Prepare students to share their educational justification.
Pilot readiness: The program can begin with a very small team, one teacher-client problem, one shared product, and a local presentation.

Choose a format

One-on-one tutoring

A teacher guides one student. Add teacher-client, family, peer, or virtual feedback so the student designs for another learner.

Small-group club

Students share one product, rotate roles, interview one another, and test across teams.

Classroom integration

Teach the curriculum in class and use class, independent, or club time for projects.

Virtual club

Use shared documents, breakout groups, clear agendas, accessible tools, and privacy-safe examples.

Lunch or short-cycle club

Reduce scope to one component and one prototype question. Use mini-lessons and templates.

Boot camp or showcase day

Use pre-work and stations for empathy synthesis, analysis, prototyping, testing, revision, and presentation.

Advisor role and boundaries

Advisors should

  • Teach or coordinate the curriculum.
  • Help students access teacher-clients and credible research.
  • Ask for evidence and reasoning.
  • Approve tools, safety, accessibility, testing, and sharing.
  • Support conflict resolution and inclusive participation.
  • Reduce scope when needed.
  • Document implementation and participant feedback.

Advisors should not

  • Select the solution.
  • Rewrite the student product.
  • Lead interviews in place of students.
  • Overvalue technical complexity.
  • Allow AI to make unsupported educational decisions.
  • Treat the showcase as the only goal.
  • Overstate impact from a small project.

Five simplified team roles

RolePrimary responsibility
Interview and Empathy LeadConversations, follow-up questions, synthesis, permission, and privacy
Learning Research LeadTask analysis, evidence, progression, myths, and design implications
Design and Prototype LeadMultiple concepts, product type, prototype, and version record
Testing and Evidence LeadTest question, observation, learning evidence, and revision
Documentation and Presentation LeadDesign notebook, contributions, rubric, educational justification, and presentation

Roles should rotate or overlap. Every student should understand the entire product. Offer oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted ways to participate.

Safety, accessibility, and family communication

  • Review age, account, platform, school, privacy, and sharing requirements.
  • Use non-identifying examples and minimize data.
  • Provide low-tech alternatives.
  • Consider reading, visual, hearing, motor, language, cognitive-load, and device access.
  • Explain what students will do and how AI will and will not be used.
  • Explain testing, events, permissions, accommodations, food, transportation, and other logistics when confirmed.
  • Provide a contact for concerns.

Use the rubric and prepare for a showcase

Students should present empathy findings, the defined problem, task analysis, learning-science evidence, product-type decision, prototype, testing, revisions, accessibility, responsible AI, impact, limitations, and team reflection.
