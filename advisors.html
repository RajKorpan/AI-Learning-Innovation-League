Start here

Your role as an advisor

You create the conditions for students to do the thinking, designing, testing, and explaining. Your job is to keep the project educationally focused, feasible, safe, accessible, and student-owned—not to choose the solution for the team.

What you will do

  • Prepare the club structure, schedule, space, technology, and local permissions.
  • Facilitate the five lessons and help students move through Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Share.
  • Ask students to explain their reasoning and connect design decisions to how learning works.
  • Help students reduce scope when a project is too broad.
  • Support inclusive participation, rotating roles, and productive teamwork.
  • Review consequential decisions with students rather than making those decisions for them.

Keep these boundaries

  • Do not select the solution or rewrite the student product.
  • Do not lead interviews in place of students.
  • Do not treat technical complexity as evidence of a stronger project.
  • Do not allow AI to make unsupported educational decisions or replace student reasoning.
  • Do not permit identifiable learner information in unapproved tools.
  • Do not coach so heavily during testing that the result no longer reflects the prototype.

Communicate with families

  • Explain the purpose of the League and that students may build an AI or non-AI learning tool.
  • Explain how AI may be used and what information students must not enter into AI systems.
  • Describe interviews, prototype testing, public sharing, privacy expectations, and any media permissions.
  • Explain how families can request an accommodation or alternative participation format.
  • Share confirmed event logistics and changes when available.
  • Direct program questions to the contact information on the Resources page.
Safety and accessibility are part of facilitation, not a separate checklist at the end.

Each lesson now includes the specific privacy, access, platform, participation, testing, or presentation questions that matter at that stage.

Plan before you teach

Build your club plan

Work through five setup decisions in order. Each step includes the guidance that used to be spread across separate readiness, format, recruitment, team, and schedule sections. The final step uses your selections to generate a customized plan.

How the planner makes recommendations

The planner is a transparent rule-based tool. It does not use generative AI, send information to a server, or save your entries.

Every selection affects at least one recommendation. Combination rules flag issues such as a virtual club without device access, a compressed schedule with students who are new to design, or advanced application development without technical support.

Use the output as a planning aid. Local school requirements and the needs of students and families take priority.

Step 1 of 6 · Confirm readiness

Step 1 · Confirm readiness

Make sure the basic conditions are in place

Do not wait until students are building to discover that a platform is not approved, a meeting space is unavailable, or a participation need has not been considered.

Confirm these before recruitment or the first meeting

  • An advisor or advisor team is identified.
  • The school has reviewed any required permissions, accounts, AI platforms, and local procedures.
  • A meeting space or virtual platform is plausible.
  • You have a low-tech path even if students later choose digital tools.
  • You have a way to learn about reading, language, visual, hearing, motor, cognitive, device, transportation, or scheduling needs without asking students to disclose more than necessary.
  • You know whom to contact if a student needs accessibility, technical, translation, or other support.

Start small: one advisor, a small student team, one focused project, one low-resolution prototype, and one local sharing event are enough for a complete first cycle.

Step 2 · Choose a format

Choose the setting that fits the students and time you actually have

Small-group team

Best default for collaborative design. Students share one product, rotate roles, critique, and test together.

One-on-one

Works for one student, but add another learner, teacher-client, family member, or reviewer so the design is not based only on the advisor's perspective.

Classroom integration

Teach the five lessons in class and use class, independent, or club time for project work.

Virtual club

Use shared documents, accessible platforms, explicit agendas, and privacy-safe examples. Confirm devices and connectivity first.

Lunch or short-cycle

Keep mini-lessons short and reduce scope to one learning component, one strategy, and one prototype question.

Boot camp or showcase day

Use substantial pre-work. The live session should focus on synthesis, prototyping, testing, revision, and presentation—not trying to do all discovery from scratch.

Step 3 · Recruit students and connect people

Know who is participating and whose learning challenge the project will address

Recruit students

  • Explain that teams can design something to help themselves learn or to help someone else learn.
  • Emphasize that students will learn about human learning and AI while they design; they do not need prior coding or AI expertise.
  • Recruit for curiosity and willingness to test ideas, not only technical skill.

Connect a teacher-client or other perspective when useful

  • Identify a teacher who can describe a difficult-to-teach concept, recurring misconception, or prerequisite students often lack.
  • Ask for a recent example, signs of confusion, current supports, and what successful performance looks like.
  • Keep the first conversation focused on understanding rather than proposing a solution.

Communicate with families

Before interviews, testing, or public sharing, explain the League purpose, possible AI use, privacy expectations, accommodations, and participation/sharing logistics that are already known.

Step 4 · Form and facilitate the team

Create a routine that keeps every student involved

Use five flexible roles

RoleMain responsibility
ListenInterviews, empathy, permission, and synthesizing learner needs.
LearningTask analysis, learning ideas, evidence, and checking assumptions.
DesignIdeas, product comparison, prototypes, and version changes.
TestTest questions, observation, learning evidence, and revision.
ShareDocumentation, project story, demonstration, and accessible presentation.

Roles should rotate or overlap. Every student should be able to explain the whole project.

Use the same meeting routine

  1. Review the current design stage and the evidence needed next.
  2. Assign or rotate roles.
  3. Complete one focused task.
  4. Pause for learning-goal, safety, and accessibility checks built into that lesson.
  5. Give one strength, one question, and one evidence-based critique.
  6. Record the decision and next action.

Keep participation inclusive

  • Offer oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted ways to contribute.
  • Separate technical building from educational decision-making.
  • Rotate high-status tasks such as building and presenting.
  • Use clear chunks, models, checklists, and flexible pacing.
  • Reduce project scope instead of excluding a student from meaningful work.
  • Critique the design, not the person.
Step 5 · Select a schedule

Match the five lessons to the time available

The student journey is Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Share. The advisor curriculum uses five lessons because Lesson 5 includes both Test and Share. Each lesson page offers a 45-minute or two-session 90-minute version.

Five-week cycle
  1. Empathize
  2. Define
  3. Ideate
  4. Prototype
  5. Test + Share

Use the 45-minute lesson plans when meeting once per week. Keep project work tightly scoped and use work time between lessons when available.

Six-week cycle
  1. Orientation/team setup + begin Empathize
  2. Complete Empathize
  3. Define
  4. Ideate
  5. Prototype + first test
  6. Test, revise, and Share
Ten-week cycle

Give roughly two meetings to Empathize, two to Define, two to Ideate/Prototype development, two to testing/revision, and one to two for Share preparation. Use the 90-minute lesson versions as two-session sequences where helpful.

Semester cycle

Use the five lessons as anchor points and add project work, additional testing, optional learning-science extensions, and more independent building between them. Do not require advanced technology simply because more time is available.

Step 6 · Review and generate

Create your customized implementation plan

The planner will combine all five setup decisions and flag combinations that need attention. The result includes recommended team structure, meeting design, calendar, technology guidance, stakeholder steps, access recommendations, and priority actions.

Before using the plan:

Review it against school requirements and confirm any still-unknown needs with students and families. The generated plan does not replace local accessibility, safety, privacy, or platform review.

Complete the five setup steps, then generate your plan.

Facilitate the League

Five lessons guide the six-stage student journey

Students experience six visible stages: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Share. Advisors use five lesson guides because the fifth lesson combines Test with preparation to Share. Each lesson page lets you choose a single 45-minute plan or a two-session 90-minute plan.

Two outcomes matter throughout.

Students design something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they develop their own understanding of how people learn and of what AI can and cannot do for learning.
