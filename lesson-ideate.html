Your goal

Your goal

Students will explore learning strategies that fit the defined breakdown, notice useful materials and people already around them, generate multiple AI and non-AI possibilities, and select a small set of ideas worth prototyping.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

Students often jump from a problem directly to a familiar product—especially a chatbot. Ideate deliberately separates what might help learning from what technology should be used.

The most important addition in this lesson is resourceful design: students inventory what they already have—paper, whiteboards, calculators, physical objects, classroom spaces, peers, devices—and ask how those resources could become part of a better learning experience.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Confirm the team has an advisor-approved focused problem statement from Define.
  • Review the top Strategy Explorer categories so you can discuss them without treating the matcher as a diagnosis.
  • Look around the actual learning environment and note materials, spaces, devices, people, and constraints students can use.
  • Prepare one example where a simple physical or social design is stronger than a chatbot.
  • Open the student Ideate stage so Strategy Explorer, Resource Inventor, and Idea Board are ready.

Core materials

Student Ideate page; Strategy Explorer; Resource Inventor; Idea Board; paper/index cards/markers; actual classroom materials students can inspect.

Try this

Try this

Core activities are the minimum League experience. Keep these even when time is short. A lesson may span more than one meeting; do not remove evidence-gathering or testing simply to fit one session.

1

Start from the learning action

5–8 min

Ask: “What should the learner actually think, notice, retrieve, explain, compare, practice, monitor, or decide?” Keep the focus on learner action, not product features.

2

Explore possible learning strategies

15–20 min

Students use Strategy Explorer. Review the top three recommendations together and discuss why they fit, what evidence is still weak, and when each strategy may not fit.

3

Run a resource hunt

10 min

Students inventory what is available: classroom supplies, notes, books, whiteboards, manipulatives, calculators, devices, spaces, partners, teachers, and other everyday resources.

4

Use Resource Inventor

10–15 min

Combine the target learning action, selected strategy, and available resources to generate starting ideas. Treat them as provocations to modify—not answers.

5

Complete the Five-Idea Sprint

15–20 min

Require variety: one no-AI idea, one using something already in the environment, one involving another person, one AI-supported idea when appropriate, and one deliberately unusual idea.

6

Shortlist without choosing the fanciest

10 min

Compare ideas using learner action, evidence, accessibility, available resources, and testability. Carry 2–3 promising ideas into Prototype rather than overcommitting now.

Just-in-time advisor learning

Open these only when you need the concept. Each is designed to take about two minutes and includes language you can immediately use with students.

2-minute background · Strategies should fit the breakdown

Key idea: Learning strategies are useful under particular conditions; there is no single best strategy for every learning problem.

Say to students: “The strategy is our theory about what kind of practice or support might change the breakdown.”

Quick example: If the learner understands a concept today but cannot recall it later, retrieval practice may fit better than another explanation.

Avoid: Choosing a strategy because it is popular or has a high match score without checking the mapped problem.

2-minute background · Constraints can create better ideas

Key idea: Design is not only about adding technology. Available materials, spaces, people, time, and access constraints can become creative resources.

Say to students: “Before asking what app you can build, ask what you already have that could change what the learner does.”

Quick example: To explore slope, a ruler, whiteboard, staircase, and partner may create a stronger first prototype than a chatbot.

Avoid: Treating low-tech or physical ideas as less advanced or less valuable.

2-minute background · AI is a role, not the goal

Key idea: AI can research, generate options, create materials, participate in an interaction, or help build a tool. None of those roles automatically makes the learning design better.

Say to students: “Use AI when it adds a capability your learning idea needs—not because the League has AI in its name.”

Quick example: AI might generate retrieval questions that students curate into a paper card game; the final tool itself is non-AI.

Avoid: Defaulting to conversational AI before deciding what the learner needs to do.

What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A strategy rationale explaining what the team thinks may help and why.
  • An inventory of useful materials, people, spaces, and technologies actually available.
  • At least 3–5 meaningfully different ideas, including non-AI possibilities.
  • A shortlist of 2–3 ideas that fit the learner action and can be prototyped quickly.
  • A reflection: What did we learn about how different learning strategies work? Where did AI help our ideation, and where was a non-AI idea stronger?

Need more?

Need more?

Do not add all of these. Choose the one that responds to what you are seeing from students.

Optional extension

Verify the strategy more deeply

Before selecting a strategy, have students locate and verify one additional source explaining when it tends to help and what limitations or boundary conditions matter.

Materials: Claim & Evidence Tracker, credible source, strategy comparison notes.

Use when students need more support

Constrain the brainstorm

Give students resource cards and strategy cards. Require exactly three ideas first: one paper/physical, one partner/social, and one digital/AI-supported idea.

Materials: printed strategy cards, resource picture cards, idea sentence starters, sample project cards.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Design under competing constraints

Ask the team to create an idea that works with no internet, an idea that supports two representations, or an idea that combines two compatible strategies without adding unnecessary complexity.

Materials: constraint cards, accessibility checklist, second strategy source.
