Understand

The idea behind the activity

A learning strategy is not a product. It describes the kind of thinking or practice that may help. AI can broaden brainstorming, but students should compare multiple ideas and keep control of the design decision.

Plan

Prepare one move for your students

Choose one learning challenge and one strategy example you can model. Identify at least three low-tech resources students could use if they start defaulting to a chatbot or app.

Your goal

Your goal

Students will learn to recognize a small set of learning-challenge types, compare strategy hypotheses, inventory resources and learner interests, use AI to generate multiple activity possibilities under real constraints, and create several meaningfully different ideas before choosing what to prototype.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

Students often jump from a problem directly to a familiar product—especially a chatbot. Ideate deliberately separates what might help learning from what technology should be used.

The most important addition in this lesson is resourceful design: students inventory what they already have—paper, whiteboards, calculators, physical objects, classroom spaces, peers, devices—and ask how those resources could become part of a better learning experience.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Confirm that the team has an advisor-approved focused problem statement from Define.
  • Review the Student Strategy Explorer, Resource Inventor, and Five-Idea Sprint.
  • Look around the room and identify actual materials, people, spaces, and technology students can use.
  • Prepare one example showing that the same learning strategy can be delivered through very different products.
  • Remind yourself not to steer students toward a chatbot or the most technical option.

Core materials

Focused problem + learning objective from Define; Student Ideate page; Strategy Explorer practice cases; Resource Inventor; classroom materials or resource cards; optional advisor-approved AI tool; paper/sticky notes for the Five-Idea Sprint.

Safety and access to plan for Ideate

  • Require at least one idea that can work without a specialized platform or paid account.
  • Ask whether different learners could access the instructions, materials, pace, and response format.
  • When students use AI for brainstorming, remove identifying learner information and treat generated ideas as possibilities, not educational evidence.
  • Do not let a technically impressive idea crowd out a simpler option that better preserves learner agency and participation.

1Name the learner action before naming a product0–5 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Read the focused problem statement aloud.
  2. Ask: What do we want the learner to do differently—recall, explain, compare, plan, interpret, practice, monitor, or something else?
  3. Write that learner action where the team can see it throughout Ideate.
  4. Challenge any product-first statement such as “we want to make a chatbot” by asking what learning action the chatbot would support.
What to explain

Strategy is not the same as product

Key idea: A learning strategy describes the kind of thinking or practice the learner needs. A product is only one way to deliver that strategy.

Student-friendly language: “First decide what kind of learning experience may help. Then invent different ways to create that experience.”

Example: Retrieval practice could be a card game, partner challenge, paper quiz, classroom routine, or guided digital tool.

Watch for: Do not let the name of a technology substitute for a learning rationale.

Finish this step with: one clear learner action the team is trying to support.
2Explore learning strategies step by step5–15 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Before students classify their own project, open the “Which challenge fits best?” practice cases. Have them explain why the category describes the learner action rather than labeling the learner.
  2. Have students enter the breakdown, learner stage, desired mental action, feedback need, evidence strength, and constraints.
  3. Read the top three Strategy Explorer results rather than accepting only the first.
  4. For each strategy, ask students to explain why it fits—or why it may not fit—the Task Map.
  5. Choose one leading strategy and one alternate to carry forward.
Open with studentsStrategy ExplorerCompare ranked strategies, reasons, cautions, and concrete product possibilities.
Finish this step with: a primary strategy plus at least one plausible alternative. Students should also be able to state what the learner would actually do, what the learning tool should do, and what evidence would show that the strategy helped.
Explain this idea here

A strategy changes what the learner does

Key idea: Choose a learning strategy because it prompts a useful mental action, not because it sounds sophisticated or works well with a favorite technology.

Say it this way: “First decide what thinking or practice could help. Then decide what to build.”

Example: If recall is the problem, retrieval practice might become cards, a partner challenge, a quiz, or an AI-guided routine.

Watch for: Do not treat a chatbot, game, or app as a learning strategy by itself.

3Turn available resources into an AI activity-design prompt15–25 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Give students two minutes to physically scan the room and list resources they can realistically use: materials, devices, people, spaces, and time.
  2. Open the Resource Inventor. Students move through one question at a time: exact learner action, learning strategy, available resources, grouping, activity length, and access requirements.
  3. Have students read the generated prompt before using it. Ask: Does this tell AI to use our real constraints? Does it preserve the learner's thinking? Does it ask for several possibilities rather than one “best” answer?
  4. Paste the prompt into an advisor-approved AI system when available. Save several useful possibilities or ingredients, but do not select one yet.
  5. If an approved AI system is not available, use the prompt as a team brainstorming challenge: each teammate invents one response to the same constraints.
  6. Add one or two learner interests only when they can make the activity more meaningful without distracting from the learning goal.
  7. Ask the AI for multiple genuinely different possibilities. Students must choose, combine, revise, or reject the ideas rather than treating the first output as the design.
Explain this idea here

AI is a brainstorming partner, not the designer of record

Key idea: A well-constructed prompt can make AI combine learning strategies with real materials and constraints, but students still evaluate whether the resulting activities actually address the learning breakdown.

Say it this way: “We are giving AI a better design brief so it can widen our options. We still decide what is educationally useful.”

Example: Ask for three comparison activities that teach slope using a whiteboard, ruler, line cards, a partner, and 15 minutes.

Watch for: Engaging AI output can still target the wrong skill or do too much of the learner’s thinking.

Use during this stepResource InventorBuild a detailed prompt to submit to an approved AI system for creative activity ideas.
Finish this step with: one reviewed AI activity-design prompt and several possibilities or ingredients to critique during the Five-Idea Sprint.
4Run the Five-Idea Sprint25–37 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Set a visible timer. The goal is variety, not polish.
  2. Require one idea with no AI, one using existing classroom resources, one social/people-based idea, one AI-supported idea, and one wild-card idea.
  3. Students should sketch or describe the learner interaction—not just name a product.
  4. After all five exist, allow students to combine the strongest pieces.
Open with studentsFive-Idea SprintCapture five meaningfully different concepts before selecting one.
Finish this step with: at least five distinct concepts that deliver the learning strategy in different ways.
5Compare the strongest possibilities37–43 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Compare the ideas against the target learner action.
  2. Ask which ideas preserve the thinking the learner needs to practice.
  3. Check accessibility, available resources, time, testability, and whether AI adds a necessary capability.
  4. Keep 2–3 ideas for Prototype instead of prematurely selecting one polished solution.
What to explain

AI can assist ideation without owning the decision

Key idea: AI can suggest formats, examples, constraints, or combinations, but the team must evaluate ideas against evidence about the learner and learning goal.

Student-friendly language: “Use AI to widen possibilities, then use your evidence to narrow them.”

Example: Ask AI for three ways to practice retrieval using only index cards and a partner. Students still decide whether any suggestion fits.

Watch for: Do not let AI-generated creativity replace interviewing, learning-science reasoning, or student judgment.

Finish this step with: a shortlist of 2–3 ideas with a sentence explaining why each might help learning.
6Advisor checkpoint43–45 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask which idea best targets the learner action and why.
  2. Ask what the team would learn by prototyping each finalist.
  3. Require at least one non-AI or low-tech possibility in the comparison.
Finish this step with: two ideas worth carrying into Prototype

What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A strategy rationale explaining what the team thinks may help and why.
  • An inventory of useful materials, people, spaces, and technologies actually available.
  • At least 3–5 meaningfully different ideas, including non-AI possibilities.
  • A shortlist of 2–3 ideas that fit the learner action and can be prototyped quickly.
  • A reflection: What did we learn about how different learning strategies work? Where did AI help our ideation, and where was a non-AI idea stronger?
  • A resource/activity-design prompt that includes available materials, learner interests when useful, constraints, accessibility needs, and a request for multiple alternatives.
Open the student Ideate stage
Between meetings

Keep the project moving without adding another full lesson

Use only what fits your students and schedule. These tasks can be completed independently, with a teammate, or during a short advisor check-in.

  1. Have students complete the Five-Idea Sprint if the team generated fewer than three genuinely different possibilities.
  2. Encourage one quick no-tech or low-tech trial before students commit to a build.
  3. Bring the top strategy, available resources, and two candidate ideas into Prototype.
  4. If students actually try one Resource Inventor activity between meetings, have them complete the Resource Inventor reflection: what happened, what the learner noticed or explained, where confusion remained, and what should change.

Next meeting: begin by asking students what they tried, what changed, and what they still need help figuring out.

Need more?

Choose only the option that responds to what you are seeing. Each adaptation has a clear purpose, a short procedure, and a stopping point so it does not turn into an extra lesson.

Optional extensionRemix two strategies and two resources

Use this when: Students have a strong initial shortlist and need more creative range.

How to run it

  1. Select two different learning strategies from Strategy Explorer.
  2. Select two available resources that the team did not use in its first ideas.
  3. Generate one concept for each strategy/resource combination.
  4. Compare which combination changes the learner’s thinking in the most useful way.
  5. Add only the strongest new concept to the shortlist.

Materials: strategy results, resource inventory, four-box remix sheet or sticky notes.

You are ready to continue when: students can explain how changing the strategy or resource changes the learning interaction.

Use when students need more supportUse strategy cards and resource picture cards

Use this when: Students repeat the same kind of idea or have difficulty imagining non-digital options.

How to run it

  1. Give the team 3–4 strategy cards selected from their top Strategy Explorer results.
  2. Spread resource cards or real objects on the table.
  3. Have the student pick one strategy card and two resource cards at random.
  4. Ask: “What could the learner do with these?” Sketch for two minutes.
  5. Repeat three times, then choose which idea best fits the focused problem.

Materials: printed strategy cards, resource picture cards or real classroom objects, blank paper, timer.

You are ready to continue when: the team has at least three genuinely different interactions, including a non-AI option.

Use when students are ready for more challengeDesign under deliberate constraints

Use this when: Students quickly generate plausible ideas and are ready to test their design assumptions.

How to run it

  1. Choose one constraint such as no screens, two minutes per interaction, multilingual directions, or use by two learners together.
  2. Redesign the leading concept under that constraint.
  3. Choose a second, very different constraint and redesign again.
  4. Compare what each constraint reveals about the essential learning interaction.
  5. Decide whether any constrained version is actually stronger than the original.

Materials: constraint cards, idea sketches, comparison matrix.

You are ready to continue when: students can identify which parts of the idea are essential and which were merely convenient features.
