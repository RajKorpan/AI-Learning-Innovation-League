What the League is and what students do

Students investigate authentic learning challenges, study how people learn, create one shared team learning tool, test it with users, and improve it through feedback.

  1. Learn about human learning and AI.
  2. Interview a teacher-client and learners.
  3. Define a specific learning challenge.
  4. Research credible learning-science evidence.
  5. Compare possible tools, including non-AI options.
  6. Build a low-cost prototype.
  7. Test, revise, reflect, and present.

How AI will and will not be used

AI mayAI will not be treated as
Support research, brainstorming, examples, practice, feedback, simulation, or reflectionAn automatic authority on learning, teaching, disability, placement, or student ability
Help build a Gem, chatbot, or application when approvedA requirement for every final product
Be studied for errors, bias, privacy, and limitationsA replacement for teachers, families, or learner thinking

Privacy and safe participation

Students should not enter into AI tools

  • Names, addresses, phone numbers, emails, passwords, or account credentials
  • Student IDs, schedules, grades, discipline records, or unapproved work samples
  • Medical, disability, counseling, immigration, financial, or other sensitive information
  • Identifying information about another student, teacher, or family
  • Photographs, recordings, or documents without permission

Interviews, testing, and sharing

ActivityExpected practice
Empathy interviewAsk permission, explain notes or recordings, allow skipping and stopping, and minimize identifiers.
Prototype testUse realistic but non-sensitive tasks and record only information needed for revision.
Public presentationRemove identifying details, confirm permissions, and use approved links and media.

How families and community members contribute

Review communication

Comment on clarity, expectations, privacy, accommodations, and responsible AI.

Share learning experiences

Describe supports, barriers, routines, and questions without selecting the student solution.

Test a prototype

Provide feedback on clarity, usefulness, trust, and accessibility when appropriate.

Attend a presentation

Ask about evidence, learner agency, testing, limitations, and next steps.

Give program feedback

Help improve safety, value, communication, logistics, and student support.

Raise concerns

Contact the program about privacy, accessibility, participation, or events.

Accessibility and accommodations

Advisors should consider reading level, visual access, hearing access, motor access, language access, cognitive load, device availability, and low-tech alternatives. Families may share accommodation needs privately with the advisor or program contact.

Presentations, events, and logistics

Dates, locations, virtual access, food, transportation, interpretation, accessibility, media permissions, and other logistics are TBD until confirmed. Information should be shared in advance.

Families may see

Frequently asked questions

Does every student build an individual tool?

No. Students generally work as one team on one shared product.

Does every project use AI?

No. AI may support research or design, or the final product may not use AI at all.

Will AI do the student’s work?

The program is designed to preserve productive struggle, explanation, reflection, and learner responsibility. Tools should ask before telling and avoid answer substitution.

How can families support the project?

Ask about the learning problem and evidence, help students reflect, test a prototype when appropriate, provide feedback, and attend a presentation.
