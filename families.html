Project purpose

Students investigate authentic learning challenges, study how people learn, design evidence-informed learning tools, test them with users, and improve them through feedback. The final tool may or may not use AI.

What students will experience

  • Learn about human learning, AI, and common misconceptions.
  • Interview a teacher-client and peer learners.
  • Analyze a specific learning challenge.
  • Research credible learning-science evidence.
  • Create one team learning tool.
  • Test, revise, reflect, and participate in a showcase.
  • Possibly be invited to provide feedback about the program; details will be explained separately.

Privacy

  • Students should not enter names, contact information, grades, schedules, medical information, passwords, or other sensitive information into AI tools.
  • Interview notes and recordings require permission.
  • Testing should use realistic but non-sensitive examples.
  • Identifying details should be removed before sharing.
  • Families should receive clear information about any surveys, interviews, or focus groups.

Do not enter into AI tools

  • Full names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, passwords, or account credentials
  • Student IDs, schedules, grades, disciplinary records, or unapproved work samples
  • Medical, disability, counseling, immigration, financial, or other sensitive information
  • Identifying information about another student, teacher, or family
  • Photographs, recordings, or documents without permission

Interview, testing, and sharing privacy

ActivityPrivacy practice
Empathy interviewAsk permission; explain note or recording use; allow skipping and stopping; minimize identifiers.
Prototype testUse realistic but non-sensitive tasks; record behavior needed for revision, not unnecessary personal data.
Participant feedbackUse program-provided forms, follow required permissions, and protect identifying information.
ShowcaseRemove names and identifying work; confirm media and sharing permissions; use approved links.
Privacy is not anonymity by default: Students and families should receive a clear explanation of what information is collected, who can see it, how it is used, and whom to contact with questions.

Accessibility and accommodations

Advisors should consider reading level, visual access, hearing access, motor access, language access, cognitive load, device availability, and low-tech alternatives. Families may share accommodation needs privately with the appropriate program contact.

Stage-by-stage checks

StageRequired checks
ResearchVerify sources; check hallucinations; distinguish evidence from suggestion; record uncertainty.
DesignProtect privacy; minimize data; consider bias; preserve learner agency; avoid answer substitution.
BuildUse age-appropriate language; state limits; include accessible interaction and alternatives; test edge cases.
TestUse non-identifying data; allow users to stop; observe harm, frustration, or exclusion; document unexpected outputs.
ShareRemove identifying details; obtain permissions; explain limitations honestly; provide access accommodations.

Accessibility prompts

Reading level

Can the intended learner understand the instructions and feedback?

Visual access

Is contrast readable? Is meaning available without color alone? Are visuals described?

Hearing access

Are captions, transcripts, or text alternatives available?

Motor access

Can the tool be used with keyboard, switch, touch, or alternative input?

Language access

Are key terms defined? Are translations or language supports needed?

Cognitive load

Are steps, choices, and displays manageable without hiding the whole task?

Device availability

Can the tool work on available devices and connections?

Low-tech alternative

Can the same learning goal be reached without the platform?

Stop and involve an advisor when

  • A user shares sensitive personal information.
  • The tool generates harmful, discriminatory, or age-inappropriate output.
  • The project makes medical, psychological, disability, placement, or other high-stakes claims.
  • The platform or sharing setting is not approved.
  • A user becomes distressed or wants to stop.
  • Students cannot explain or verify an important claim.

Possible formats

School showcase

Teams present to peers, teachers, administrators, and families.

Virtual exchange

Teams from different settings test one another’s prototypes and give structured feedback.

Family and community event

Families review transparency, safety, accessibility, value, and student support.

Hunter College event

Students may connect with faculty, staff, undergraduates, and college pathways.

Boot camp and showcase

Teams complete a focused prototype, test, revision, and presentation sequence.

Competition-level event

Teams present at Prospective Prototyping, Product Showcase, or Educational Justification level.

Community co-design at the event

  • Teacher-clients review whether the product addresses the original need.
  • Learners describe clarity, usefulness, frustration, and access.
  • Families provide feedback on transparency, privacy, accommodations, and value.
  • Peers identify evidence, assumptions, and revision opportunities.
  • Judges reward educational quality—not technical complexity alone.

Feedback and contact

Families may be invited to complete a feedback survey about transparency, safety, accessibility, value, student support, and event experience. Contact information should be posted before public launch.
