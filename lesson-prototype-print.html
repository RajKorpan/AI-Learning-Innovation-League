Lesson 4 · Prototype

Build something small

Back to lesson

Lesson purpose

Help students choose a concrete form that fits the learning interaction and build only enough to learn what should change next.

Student stage: Prototype

Materials

  • student Ideate outputs
  • prototype materials
  • paper, cards, markers, devices as approved
  • approved AI tool if building an AI learning tool

Before the session

Try the activity yourself, read the short explanation, and decide what adaptations your students may need.

Advisor preparation

Try the student activity

  • Try choosing the smallest thing that can answer a design question. Then notice when AI is actually needed in the learner-facing experience.
  • Choose one, then check what you notice.
  • Compare these two AI prompts: “Teach slope” and “Ask the learner to predict which line is steeper, explain why, measure rise/run, then give one hint only after an attempt.” Notice what the second prompt specifies about learner thinking.

Why this matters

  • Where did you want to jump to a solution?
  • What information did you realize you were missing?
  • What might a student need modeled rather than merely explained?
  • A prototype is an experiment, not a miniature final product. Productive struggle means the learner attempts meaningful thinking before help arrives. AI belongs only when its capabilities improve the learning interaction.

Plan your support

  • Choose one project idea you can use to model “What are we trying to learn from this prototype?” Be ready to steer students toward the least complicated product form that can answer that question.

Student meeting: 1 × 45-minute meeting

1. Choose the interaction before the technology 0–5 minutes

What to do

  1. Open the student Example Learning Tools and compare at least two very different products.
  2. Ask students to name what the learner actually does in each example and what AI does—or does not do.
  3. Introduce the three build paths: AI helps make a learning experience; reusable AI learning tool; advanced optional build.
  4. For each shortlisted idea, describe what the learner does first, what happens next, and what important thinking stays with the learner.
  5. Eliminate forms that add complexity without improving the learning interaction.

2. Use the Guided Build Planner to compare product forms 5–20 minutes

What to do

  1. Open the Guided Build Planner.
  2. Students answer one question at a time about learner action, feedback, conversation, time, technical experience, access, platform permission, and data.
  3. Compare the recommended concrete forms rather than automatically accepting the first result.
  4. Select one form and confirm the technical pathway that fits it.
  5. After the team chooses a direction, tell students they will use the Learning League Design Check before polishing.

Optional extension Compare two prototype forms

Use this when: The team is uncertain whether the same learning idea is better as a physical, social, or digital interaction. How to run it Pick one learning interaction and represent it in two very small forms—for example cards and a mock chatbot. Use the same test question for both versions. Role-play both versions with the same teammate or peer. Compare which version preserves learner thinking, is easier to access, and produces clearer evidence. Choose one to carry into formal testing. Materials: paper/card materials, mock conversation or wireframe, one comparison sheet. 3. Plan the smallest prototype 20–30 minutes

What to do

  1. Continue in the same Guided Build Planner.
  2. Name the one question the prototype must answer.
  3. Specify what the learner must attempt before receiving support.
  4. List only the materials/features needed for that test and name what the team will deliberately postpone.

4. Build and role-play the interaction 30–40 minutes

What to do

  1. Create the minimum version using paper, cards, a storyboard, a sample routine, a mock conversation, or a very small digital build.
  2. Have one teammate act as the learner and another act as the tool/facilitator.
  3. Notice where the learner has to think and where the prototype may be doing too much for them.
  4. Make one quick revision before external testing.
  5. Before the advisor checkpoint, run the short Learning League Design Check . For an AI-supported product, include the “more than a one-time answer?” and “did AI do too much?” checks.

5. Advisor checkpoint 40–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask the team to demonstrate the interaction with the simplest available materials.
  2. Ask what specific design question this version can answer.
  3. If the team cannot explain what the learner does, send them back to the planner before polishing.

Student meeting: 2 × 45-minute meetings

Session 1 — 45 minutes

1. Revisit the learner interaction 0–5 minutes

What to do

  1. For each shortlisted idea, describe exactly what the learner would do first, what happens next, and what thinking should remain with the learner.
  2. Compare concrete forms such as printable resource, physical game, practice routine, interactive activity, AI-generated materials, conversational AI, role-play, multimedia, or advanced app.
  3. Eliminate forms that add complexity without improving the intended learning interaction.

2. Use the Guided Build Planner to choose a form and pathway 5–20 minutes

What to do

  1. Work through the planner one question at a time.
  2. Compare the top recommended forms with the team’s two Ideate finalists.
  3. Select the form that best supports the learner action under real access/time constraints.
  4. Confirm the technical pathway; remind students that pathways are not ranks.
  5. After the team chooses a direction, tell students they will use the Learning League Design Check before polishing.

3. Finish the low-resolution prototype plan 20–30 minutes

What to do

  2. Name the learner attempt that must happen before help.
  3. List materials and access needs.
  4. Name features the team will not build yet.
  5. Choose the first tester and observation target.

Session 2 — 45 minutes

5. Role-play, inspect, and revise the interaction 0–15 minutes

What to do

  1. Run the prototype with one teammate as learner and one as the tool/system.
  2. Stop whenever the learner’s next action is unclear.
  3. Simplify instructions, hints, pacing, or materials before adding features.

6. Run a micro-test and revise 15–25 minutes

What to do

  1. Run the Learning League Design Check before the micro-test.
  2. If AI is involved, ask explicitly whether it is doing any thinking the learner needs to practice.
  3. Have one person try the smallest interaction without coaching from the builder.
  4. Observe one question: can they understand what to do and perform the target thinking?
  5. Make one revision based on what happened.

Students produce: one evidence-based revision to the prototype

7. Use Prompt Builder only if conversation is essential 25–40 minutes

What to do

  1. Use this branch only when the learning interaction genuinely depends on reusable conversational AI.
  2. Before opening Prompt Builder, have students use Experience a designed AI learning interaction : try one example, notice the learner's role, inspect the prompt, and change one feature.
  3. Show the Prompt Builder visual explaining why learner, breakdown, strategy, feedback, activity, and evidence all change the tool design.
  4. Have students write the first prompt they would try, complete Guided Draft 1, test it, record what happened, and create Draft 2.
  5. Test Draft 2 and compare what improved rather than stopping after one revision.
  6. If AI only helps create materials, skip conversational Prompt Builder and return to the learner-facing prototype after verifying the generated content.

Students produce: a tested conversational interaction—or a justified decision to skip conversational AI.

8. Advisor checkpoint 40–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask what the prototype is testing.
  2. Ask why this product form is better than a simpler alternative for this learning interaction.
  3. Check privacy, access, and platform requirements before intended-learner testing.

Students produce: approval to move into formal Test

What students should have at the end

  • A selected build direction and a reason it fits the learning objective.
  • A low-resolution prototype plan that identifies what the learner will do and what the team is deliberately not building yet.
  • A completed learning-design check before investing in polish.
  • For conversational-AI projects only: a first prompt/draft ready to test.

Keep the project moving without adding another full lesson

  • Have students finish only the minimum version needed for testing; postpone polish and extra features.
  • Confirm any platform, privacy, accessibility, or account requirements before an AI-supported prototype is used with another learner.
  • If conversational AI is part of the design, have students complete and test Prompt Draft 1 before the next meeting.
