Your goal

Your goal

Students will choose a product family and concrete prototype form based on the learning goal, create the smallest testable version, preserve productive learner thinking, and use AI only when it adds a justified capability.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

A prototype is an experiment, not a miniature final product. The team should build the least expensive version that lets them observe the learning interaction.

This lesson also keeps technical complexity in perspective. A printable activity, physical game, practice routine, role-play, AI-generated resource, conversational AI tool, multimedia resource, or advanced app can all be legitimate outcomes. The best form is the one that fits the learning problem and can be tested safely.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Confirm that the team has 2–3 shortlisted ideas from Ideate and has not already committed to a polished product.
  • Open the student Choose Your Build, Prototype Planner, and the Prompt Builder branch if conversational AI may be appropriate.
  • Gather low-resolution prototyping materials before opening development platforms: paper, cards, sticky notes, markers, sample content, or simple wireframing materials.
  • Confirm which technology platforms are approved and what account, privacy, and accessibility constraints apply.
  • Prepare one example of a very small prototype that answers one question without representing the final product.

Core materials

Shortlisted ideas; Student Prototype page; Choose Your Build; paper/cards/markers; Prototype Planner; approved technology only when needed.

How to run each step

Open the details as you reach each activity. Explanations and student tools are placed at the moment you need them.

1Choose the interaction before the technology5–8 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. For each shortlisted idea, describe exactly what the learner would do first, what happens next, and what thinking should remain with the learner.
  2. Compare concrete forms such as printable resource, physical game, practice routine, interactive activity, AI-generated materials, conversational AI, role-play, multimedia, or advanced app.
  3. Eliminate forms that add complexity without improving the intended learning interaction.
What to explain

When AI belongs in the product

Key idea: AI is useful when the design genuinely benefits from variable language, adaptive conversation, generation, simulation, or another capability a simpler tool cannot provide as well.

Student-friendly language: “First justify the interaction. Then decide whether AI is needed to deliver it.”

Example: A role-play chatbot may benefit from varied responses; a fixed matching card game probably does not need AI.

Watch for: Do not use AI where outputs cannot be checked, privacy requirements cannot be met, or a simpler design does the job better.

Finish this step with: a clear description of the learner interaction before a build platform is selected.
2Use Choose Your Build to compare product forms8–12 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Enter the learner action, feedback/conversation needs, time, technical experience, technology access, accessibility needs, permissions, and available resources.
  2. Review the ranked concrete product forms rather than treating the top suggestion as a command.
  3. Discuss any blocked or risky options with the team.
  4. Select one primary form and one simpler backup.

3Choose a build path—not a rank5 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Review Paths A–D on the student page.
  2. Ask which pathway matches the product the team has already justified.
  3. Remind students that Path D is not a higher-quality project than Path A.
  4. If the chosen path requires a platform or advanced development, confirm advisor approval before proceeding.
Finish this step with: the lightest technical pathway that can deliver the intended interaction.
4Plan the smallest version that can answer one question8–12 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Write one test question such as “Can the learner connect physical steepness to rise/run?”
  2. List only the pieces that must exist for someone to try that interaction.
  3. Explicitly list what the team will not build yet.
  4. Name the main accessibility or safety risk and a low-tech/backup option.
What to explain

A prototype is an experiment

Key idea: The purpose of a prototype is to answer a design question. It does not need all planned features or polished presentation.

Student-friendly language: “Build only enough to learn what to change next.”

Example: Before coding a tutor, role-play five turns of the conversation on paper and see whether the hint sequence supports the learner.

Watch for: Avoid spending time on branding, navigation polish, or features that do not help answer the current test question.

Open with studentsPrototype PlannerDefine the one question, minimum build, access plan, and test situation.
Finish this step with: a prototype plan with one test question and a clear “not building yet” list.
5Build and role-play before polishing15–25 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Create the minimum version using paper, cards, a storyboard, a sample routine, a mock conversation, or a very small digital build.
  2. Have one teammate act as the learner and another act as the tool/facilitator.
  3. Notice where the learner has to think and where the prototype may be doing too much for them.
  4. Make one quick revision before external testing.
What to explain

Preserve productive struggle

Key idea: Support should help the learner make progress while preserving the thinking that produces learning.

Student-friendly language: “If the tool does the important thinking for the learner, it may solve the task without supporting learning.”

Example: Instead of giving the operation for a word problem, ask the learner to identify givens, unknowns, and relationships, then offer a hint if needed.

Watch for: Watch for answer substitution disguised as “help.”

Finish this step with: a low-resolution version another person can actually try.
6Open Prompt Builder only when conversation is essentialOptional branch within the 90-minute versionDetails

How to do it

  1. Use this branch only if the product depends on a conversational AI interaction.
  2. Have students write the first prompt they would try before opening the guided questions.
  3. Complete the builder, compare the original prompt with Draft 1, test a small interaction, and revise.
  4. If the product only uses AI to create content, generate a small sample, verify it, and return to the non-conversational prototype.

Finish this step with: a tested conversational interaction—or a justified decision to skip conversational AI.
What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A justified broad product family and a concrete prototype form.
  • A prototype test question and a clear statement of what is intentionally not being built yet.
  • A low-resolution prototype that another person can actually try.
  • Defined safeguards, accessibility considerations, and a low-tech or backup pathway.
  • For AI products, a plan for verifying outputs and preserving productive struggle.
  • A reflection: What did prototyping teach us about learning? What did we learn about when AI adds value versus when it adds complexity?

Need more?

Need more?

Choose only the option that responds to what you are seeing. Each adaptation has a clear purpose, a short procedure, and a stopping point so it does not turn into an extra lesson.

Optional extension

Compare two prototype forms

Use this when: The team is uncertain whether the same learning idea is better as a physical, social, or digital interaction.

How to run it

  1. Pick one learning interaction and represent it in two very small forms—for example cards and a mock chatbot.
  2. Use the same test question for both versions.
  3. Role-play both versions with the same teammate or peer.
  4. Compare which version preserves learner thinking, is easier to access, and produces clearer evidence.
  5. Choose one to carry into formal testing.

Materials: paper/card materials, mock conversation or wireframe, one comparison sheet.

You are ready to continue when: the team can justify the selected form using evidence from the two low-resolution versions.

Use when students need more support

Prototype on paper before opening technology

Use this when: Students are stuck in platform decisions, want to build too much, or cannot describe the learner interaction.

How to run it

  1. Close development platforms temporarily.
  2. Give the team a one-question prototype card: “What do we need to learn from this version?”
  3. Provide paper interface pieces, cards, arrows, or role cards.
  4. Build only the first 3–5 learner actions or conversation turns.
  5. Role-play it once, circle what was confusing, and revise before deciding whether technology is still needed.

Materials: storyboard template, paper buttons/cards, role cards, sample low-resolution prototype, one-question test card.

You are ready to continue when: students can demonstrate the core interaction without needing to explain unfinished technical features.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Compare versions or take an advanced build path

Use this when: The core interaction already works at low resolution and the team can explain why more technical complexity is useful.

How to run it

  1. Write 3–5 test cases, including an expected case and at least one edge case.
  2. Implement a second version, more independent prompt, or small advanced build.
  3. Run the same test cases on both versions.
  4. Check output verification, privacy, accessibility, and what thinking remains with the learner.
  5. Keep the advanced version only if it improves the learning interaction or testability.

Materials: approved development platform, edge-case checklist, test cases, version-comparison sheet.

You are ready to continue when: students can show what the added technical complexity improves and what new risks it introduces.
