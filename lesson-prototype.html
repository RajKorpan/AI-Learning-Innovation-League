Your goal

Your goal

Students will choose a product family and concrete prototype form based on the learning goal, create the smallest testable version, preserve productive learner thinking, and use AI only when it adds a justified capability.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

A prototype is an experiment, not a miniature final product. The team should build the least expensive version that lets them observe the learning interaction.

This lesson also keeps technical complexity in perspective. A printable activity, physical game, practice routine, role-play, AI-generated resource, conversational AI tool, multimedia resource, or advanced app can all be legitimate outcomes. The best form is the one that fits the learning problem and can be tested safely.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Review the team's 2–3 shortlisted ideas and the learning action each is meant to support.
  • Confirm approved technology, age/account requirements, privacy expectations, and available devices.
  • Gather low-resolution materials: paper, index cards, markers, sticky notes, printed interface pieces, or a shared document.
  • Review the student Choose Your Build and Prototype Planner.
  • Do not open Prompt Builder unless the team has justified a conversational-AI interaction.

Core materials

Student Prototype page; Choose Your Build; Prototype Planner; paper/storyboard supplies; selected resources; approved technology only when needed.

Try this

Try this

Core activities are the minimum League experience. Keep these even when time is short. A lesson may span more than one meeting; do not remove evidence-gathering or testing simply to fit one session.

1

Name the interaction before the product

5–8 min

Ask: “What does the learner need to do, and what response or environment will help them do it?” Have students describe the interaction without naming a platform.

2

Choose a broad product family and concrete form

12–15 min

Use Choose Your Build. Keep the four broad families for reasoning, then translate the recommendation into a concrete prototype such as a printable resource, physical game, practice routine, interactive activity, AI-generated materials, conversational AI, role-play, AI-supported physical activity, multimedia resource, or advanced app.

3

Choose the lightest technical pathway

5–8 min

Remind teams that build paths are alternatives, not rankings. A more technical product is not automatically stronger.

4

Plan the smallest useful prototype

10–15 min

Students use Prototype Planner to name one test question, what must exist for that test, what can remain unbuilt, the main risk, and an accessible low-tech alternative.

5

Build and role-play

20–30 min

Create the minimum version. Before polishing, have one teammate act as the learner and another act as the tool or facilitator.

6

Branch only when needed

15+ min optional within core path

If conversational AI is essential, move into Guided Prompt Builder. If AI only helps create content, generate a small sample and verify it. If the idea is physical/non-AI, skip Prompt Builder entirely.

Just-in-time advisor learning

Open these only when you need the concept. Each is designed to take about two minutes and includes language you can immediately use with students.

2-minute background · Prototype as experiment

Key idea: A prototype exists to answer a question about the design. It does not need all planned features.

Say to students: “Build only enough to learn what to change next.”

Quick example: Before coding a tutor, role-play five turns of the conversation on paper and see whether the hint sequence supports the learner.

Avoid: Polishing screens, branding, or features that do not help answer the current test question.

2-minute background · Productive struggle

Key idea: Support should help learners make progress while preserving the thinking that produces learning.

Say to students: “If the tool does the important thinking for the learner, it may solve the task without supporting learning.”

Quick example: Instead of giving the operation for a word problem, ask the learner to identify givens, unknowns, and relationships, then offer a hint when needed.

Avoid: Answer substitution disguised as “help.”

2-minute background · When AI belongs in the product

Key idea: AI is most useful when the design genuinely benefits from variable language, adaptive conversation, generation, simulation, or another capability that simpler tools cannot provide as well.

Say to students: “First justify the interaction. Then decide whether AI is needed to deliver it.”

Quick example: AI may be useful for a role-play conversation with varied responses; it is unnecessary for a fixed matching card game.

Avoid: Using AI where outputs cannot be verified, privacy requirements cannot be met, or a simpler design does the job better.

What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A justified broad product family and a concrete prototype form.
  • A prototype test question and a clear statement of what is intentionally not being built yet.
  • A low-resolution prototype that another person can actually try.
  • Defined safeguards, accessibility considerations, and a low-tech or backup pathway.
  • For AI products, a plan for verifying outputs and preserving productive struggle.
  • A reflection: What did prototyping teach us about learning? What did we learn about when AI adds value versus when it adds complexity?

Need more?

Need more?

Do not add all of these. Choose the one that responds to what you are seeing from students.

Optional extension

Compare two prototype forms

Build two very small versions of the same learning idea in different forms—such as cards versus chatbot turns—and identify what each makes easier or harder to test.

Materials: paper storyboard, sample content, simple comparison sheet.

Use when students need more support

Prototype before technology

Require a storyboard, paper conversation, card deck, or role-play before opening any development platform. Give students reusable interface pieces and a one-question test template.

Materials: storyboard template, paper buttons/cards, role cards, sample prototype, “what are we testing?” card.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Build a second version or advanced path

After the core interaction works on paper, advanced teams may implement prompt behavior more independently, code a small app, or compare two versions. Require the same learning rationale, verification, accessibility, and test plan.

Materials: approved development platform, edge-case checklist, test cases, version-comparison sheet.
