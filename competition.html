Competition and Showcases

The League recognizes different stages of work and different product types. Empathy, evidence, alignment, testing, accessibility, and reflection matter more than technical sophistication.

On this pageLevelsProduct tracksRubricEducational justificationPresentationEventsFull detailed guides

Three competition levels

Level 1: Prospective Prototyping

Present: empathy findings, defined problem, task analysis, learning-science review, proposed design, and low-resolution prototype.

Level 2: Product Showcase

Present: completed or partial tool, testing evidence, feedback, revisions, and early evidence of usefulness.

Level 3: Educational Justification

Explain: problem choice, evidence, product format, productive struggle, feedback, impact, limitations, and next steps.

Product tracks

AI-assisted non-AI tool

AI may support research, design, or content, while the final tool is paper, physical, visual, game-based, or activity-based.

Gem or chatbot

The final tool uses conversational AI for practice, reflection, debate, question decomposition, or error analysis.

Advanced custom AI application

The team builds a custom application when the added technology clearly serves the learning goal.

Educational justification

  1. Why did the team select this teacher-client and learner problem?
  2. What is the smallest meaningful component?
  3. What learning-science evidence informed the design?
  4. Why was this product format appropriate?
  5. How did the tool preserve productive struggle?
  6. How did feedback work and fade?
  7. How did testing change the product or problem?
  8. What evidence suggests usefulness?
  9. What responsible AI and accessibility decisions were made?
  10. What limitations remain?

Competition levels

Level 1: Prospective Prototyping

Students present: empathy findings, defined problem, task analysis, learning-science review, proposed design, and low-resolution prototype.

Primary question: Is the proposed learning tool grounded in a real need and credible evidence?

Level 2: Product Showcase

Students present: completed or partially completed tool, testing evidence, feedback, revisions, and early evidence of usefulness.

Primary question: What happened when people used the tool, and how did the team respond?

Level 3: Educational Justification

Students formally explain: problem choice, learning-science evidence, product-format decision, productive struggle, feedback, impact, limitations, and next steps.

Primary question: Can the team defend the educational design and evidence?

Advancement is not based on technology alone

  • A paper tool can score highly at every level.
  • A custom application does not automatically qualify for a higher level.
  • Teams may remain at a level while improving evidence.
  • Judges should identify what would be required for the next level.
  • Safety, accessibility, and student ownership apply at all levels.

Product tracks

AI-assisted non-AI tool

AI supports research, brainstorming, or content drafting, but the final product is paper-based, physical, visual, game-based, or activity-based.

Gem or chatbot

The final tool uses conversational AI to guide practice, reflection, debate, question decomposition, error analysis, or another learning action.

Advanced custom AI application

A team builds a custom application or workflow in Google AI Studio or another approved environment.

Track decision questions

  • What problem is being solved?
  • What must the learner do?
  • What does the technical feature make possible?
  • Could a simpler product work better?
  • What new risks or access barriers appear?
  • Can the team test the product realistically?
  • Can the team explain and document the AI contribution?

Use the rubric throughout the cycle

Teams should self-assess after problem definition, before testing, and before the showcase. Advisors and judges should give evidence-based comments and a next-level recommendation.

Educational justification

Required argument

  1. Why did the team select this teacher-client and learner problem?
  2. What is the smallest meaningful component, and how was it identified?
  3. What learning-science evidence informed the design?
  4. Why was this product format more appropriate than alternatives?
  5. How does the tool preserve productive struggle and learner agency?
  6. What feedback is provided, when, and how does it fade?
  7. How did testing change the product or problem definition?
  8. What evidence suggests usefulness or impact?
  9. What privacy, bias, accessibility, and responsible-AI decisions were made?
  10. What limitations remain, and what would the next cycle test?

Evidence versus claim

Weak statementStronger educational justification
“Students liked the game.”“Four learners completed more retrieval attempts, but two relied on recognition cues; the next version will reduce cues gradually.”
“AI personalized it.”“The Gem selected a graduated hint based on the learner’s explanation; testing found one incorrect inference, so the team added verification and a teacher-review rule.”
“It is for visual learners.”“The graph annotation makes scale relationships explicit and provides text descriptions for access; the representation matches the content and identified bottleneck.”
Presentation guidance

Recommended project story

  1. Introduce the teacher-client need and intended learner without identifying information.
  2. Share empathy findings and what assumptions changed.
  3. Define the smallest meaningful component and show the task analysis.
  4. Explain the learning-science evidence and its strength.
  5. Show the options considered and why the selected tool format fit.
  6. Demonstrate the low-resolution and later prototype.
  7. Present testing evidence: what users said and what they actually did.
  8. Explain revisions, responsible AI, accessibility, impact, and limitations.
  9. Reflect on team learning and the next design cycle.

Presentation best practices

Lead with the human need

Start with the teacher-client and learner experience—not the platform.

Use evidence visually

Show an interview insight, task map, evidence record, prototype comparison, or observation.

Demonstrate briefly

Use one prepared interaction and a backup screenshot or transcript.

Share speaking roles

Every team member contributes and can answer questions about the whole project.

Explain productive struggle

Show what the learner must still think, decide, practice, or reflect on.

Name limitations

Describe errors, untested contexts, access barriers, and what the tool should not do.

Protect privacy

Remove names, account information, sensitive examples, and identifying media.

Design accessible materials

Use large text, strong contrast, captions, descriptive headings, and verbal descriptions.

Prepare for failure

Have a non-live demonstration and a plan if the technology is unavailable.

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the sharing stage

  • Remove identifying details and obtain permissions.
  • Do not publicly share unapproved links, recordings, or learner work.
  • Explain AI use and limitations honestly.
  • Provide captions, readable slides, language access, and alternative formats.
  • Do not overstate impact from a small number of users or tests.
  • Credit sources, collaborators, and community contributors.

Possible formats

School showcase

Teams present to peers, teachers, administrators, and families.

Virtual exchange

Teams from different settings test one another’s prototypes and give structured feedback.

Family and community event

Families review transparency, safety, accessibility, value, and student support.

Hunter College event

Students may connect with faculty, staff, undergraduates, and college pathways.

Boot camp and showcase

Teams complete a focused prototype, test, revision, and presentation sequence.

Competition-level event

Teams present at Prospective Prototyping, Product Showcase, or Educational Justification level.

Community co-design at the event

  • Teacher-clients review whether the product addresses the original need.
  • Learners describe clarity, usefulness, frustration, and access.
  • Families provide feedback on transparency, privacy, accommodations, and value.
  • Peers identify evidence, assumptions, and revision opportunities.
  • Judges reward educational quality—not technical complexity alone.

What the League judges differently

  • Empathy and user understanding
  • Smallest meaningful component
  • Learning-science evidence
  • Alignment between need and design
  • Productive struggle and learner agency
  • Testing behavior, not only satisfaction
  • Accessibility and responsible AI
