Your project map

Move through six stages

0 of 6 completeStart with Empathize

Each stage is a mission. Complete the work, save the artifact, and use an advisor checkpoint when the decision needs another perspective. The process is iterative—you can return to an earlier stage whenever testing changes what you understand.

1EmpathizeUnderstand the ChallengeListen before you solve. Learn what the learner experiences, what the teacher notices, and what matters to them.You leave with: Empathy insightsStart here2DefineFind the BreakdownTurn a broad concern into a specific learning breakdown that one project can address.You leave with: Focused learning challengeComing later3IdeateInvent PossibilitiesExplore learning strategies, use resources already around you, and create several very different ideas.You leave with: A short list of promising ideasComing later4PrototypeBuild Something SmallChoose one idea and build the smallest version that can teach you whether the learning interaction works.You leave with: Testable prototypeComing later5TestPut It to the TestWatch someone use the prototype, collect evidence, and revise based on what actually happens.You leave with: Testing evidence and revisionComing later6ShareTell the StoryPrepare to explain the challenge, your reasoning, your tool, what changed through testing, and what you would improve next.You leave with: Sharing-event story and demoComing later
This is progress, not a ranking. Moving forward means your project has enough evidence to make the next decision. A simple, well-tested project is stronger than a complicated project that does not fit the learner.
Every stage has two kinds of learning.

Project learning: what helps this learner? League learning: what did your team learn about human learning and AI while making the decision?

Choose your design lens

Who are you trying to help learn?

League projects can start with a challenge you experience yourself or a challenge experienced by someone else. Either way, use evidence rather than assumptions.

Current project lens: Help someone else learn

A term you will see

Who is the teacher-client?

Your teacher may be your first client. A client is the person whose learning problem you are trying to understand and help solve. A teacher-client may tell you about something their students often struggle to learn. Your advisor may also be your teacher-client—you do not need to find a separate teacher.

Your job is not simply to build whatever the teacher asks for. You listen, investigate where learning gets hard, and design something that may help.

Scaffolding that can fade

Choose how much guidance you want

Your team can use more support at the beginning and less support as you become more independent. This is separate from how technically complicated your final product is.

Current choice: Guided

Your project does not have to be an app

Three ways your team might build

You will choose later, after you understand the learning problem. These are options—not rankings.

1

Make a learning experience

Use AI to help design a game, card deck, printable activity, experiment, role-play, practice routine, movement activity, or physical learning aid.

2

Build an AI learning tool

Create a reusable conversational experience that asks, responds, gives feedback, and keeps the learner doing the important thinking.

+

Go further

If your team already has the technical experience, you can explore a more advanced digital build with your advisor. More technology does not mean a better project.

Model League projects

See how a small project can grow from a real problem

These are examples to learn from, not projects to copy. Each one moves through the same process but ends in a different kind of learning experience.

Physical + AI-assisted design

Slope and steepness

  1. Teacher problemStudents can calculate slope but do not connect it to steepness.
  2. Interview clueThe learner says the formula works when numbers are given but the graph still feels disconnected.
  3. BreakdownConnecting rise/run as a ratio to the visual direction and steepness of a line.
  4. StrategyCompare examples and connect representations.
  5. PrototypeRuler + line cards + whiteboard activity; AI helps brainstorm variations and reflection questions.
  6. Test + revisionThe learner predicts steepness better but confuses negative slope, so the team adds contrast cases.

Printable practice

Biology vocabulary

  1. Teacher problemA student recognizes vocabulary while rereading but cannot recall it later.
  2. Interview clueThe learner studies mostly by looking over definitions again.
  3. BreakdownRetrieving a term or meaning without seeing it first.
  4. StrategyRetrieval practice with varied cues.
  5. PrototypeA partner card challenge; AI proposes practice variations that students verify and curate.
  6. Test + revisionSome cards give too much of a hint, so the team changes the cue progression.

Reusable AI learning tool

Understanding word problems

  1. Teacher problemA learner starts calculating before identifying what the problem is asking.
  2. Interview clueThe learner can calculate once a plan is clear but rushes into operations.
  3. BreakdownIdentifying givens, unknowns, and relationships before choosing an operation.
  4. StrategyBreak the task into steps and prompt self-explanation.
  5. PrototypeA conversational coach that asks questions without solving the problem.
  6. Test + revisionThe first version asks too many questions, so the team shortens the sequence and adds a reflection check.

Before you begin

Work as one team

Roles help divide responsibility, but they are not permanent jobs. Rotate, overlap, and make sure everyone can explain the whole project.
