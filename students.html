One project journey

Work through these actions as a team. The process is iterative: testing may send you back to an earlier action.

UnderstandListenDiagnoseResearchImagineBuildTestImproveShare

Select a step to move directly to that part of the project. Testing may send your team back to an earlier step.

1. Understand human learning and AI

  • An answer is not the same as learning.
  • Learning tools should preserve productive struggle, explanation, reflection, metacognition, and self-regulation.
  • Prior knowledge and cognitive load may explain where a learner gets stuck.
  • Feedback should be specific and useful without taking over.
  • AI can be a research partner, design assistant, building platform, delivery mechanism, or object of critical study.
  • The best final product may not use AI.
Learning myth check: Do not label learners as visual, auditory, or kinesthetic. Match the design to the content, task, learner difficulty, accessibility need, and evidence.

2. Listen through empathy interviews

An empathy interview is a conversation—not a script, survey, or sales pitch.

Teacher-client interview
  • Tell us about the last time students got stuck.
  • What can students already do?
  • What do successful students do differently?
  • What signs show confusion?
  • What support is currently used?
  • Can you show us an example?
  • What would a short intervention make possible?
Peer learner interview
  • What are you trying to do?
  • What do you already understand?
  • Walk us through your process.
  • Where do you hesitate or get stuck?
  • What do you try next?
  • What support helps or gets in the way?
  • How do you decide whether an answer makes sense?
Interview practices
  • Prepare starter questions but follow interesting responses.
  • Ask “Why?”, “Can you tell me more?”, and “Can you show me?”
  • Listen for stories, emotions, needs, strategies, barriers, and workarounds.
  • Ask permission before notes or recordings.
  • Do not lead the interviewee toward a preferred solution.
  • Practice with a teammate or chatbot role-play, but do not replace real stakeholder input.

3. Diagnose the smallest meaningful component

Examples of teacher-client problems

Reading comprehensionBreaking down test questionsInterpreting data tablesConverting unitsGraphing resultsDivision in scienceProportional reasoningRetrieving background knowledgeVocabularyManaging complex tasks

Move from broad to precise

Too broad

“Students are bad at science.”

Better

“Students struggle to interpret the axes and scale of a graph.”

More precise

“Students can identify the variables but cannot determine the interval represented between labeled points.”

Task analysis questions

  • What is the final learning goal?
  • What must the learner know and do?
  • What prerequisite knowledge, vocabulary, concepts, procedures, and decisions are required?
  • What common errors appear?
  • Where may cognitive load become excessive?
  • Which component is the likely bottleneck?
  • What evidence would show it has been learned?

4. Research how people learn

Investigate skill development, misconceptions, effective practice, memory, retrieval, feedback, motivation, metacognition, self-regulation, transfer, prior knowledge, cognitive load, and accessibility.

Evidence ratingMeaning
Strong research supportMultiple credible studies or syntheses support the claim in relevant contexts.
Mixed or limited supportEvidence is incomplete, context-dependent, or only partly relevant.
Unsupported claimThe claim lacks a verifiable source or is generated without evidence.
Learning mythA familiar claim should not guide design as established science.

Map the learning progression

  • What comes before this skill?
  • What does it make possible?
  • What related skills are often confused with it?
  • What does beginning, developing, and proficient performance look like?
  • How does it appear in advanced work?

The tool uses conversational AIGem, chatbot, role-play partner, debate partner, reflection coach
The tool uses advanced developmentCustom application or simulation
The best solution does not use AIManipulative, card sort, worksheet, board game, visual scaffold

Decision questions

  • What must the learner do?
  • Does AI add genuine value?
  • Could a simpler tool work better?
  • What risks or access barriers does AI introduce?
  • Can the tool be tested safely and realistically?
  • How will support fade?

6. Build a low-resolution prototype first

Build only enough to answer one question. Use a paper conversation flow, storyboard, role-play, flashcard sample, prompt skeleton, mock chatbot transcript, one-question test, clickable wireframe, or small set of sample interactions.

Every prototype should answer a question

  • Does the learner understand what to do?
  • Does feedback help without giving away the answer?
  • Does the learner stay engaged?
  • Does the tool target the intended component?
  • Can the teacher-client use it without extensive explanation?
  • Does AI add value compared with a simpler tool?
When building a Gem
  1. Use an existing Gem and identify strengths and limitations.
  2. Modify one behavior and test the change.
  3. Name a new Gem for the learning action.
  4. Use the Prompt Builder to define the learner, need, component, bottleneck, evidence, productive struggle, feedback, reflection, fading, accessibility, and limits.
  5. Add example interactions.
  6. Test answer requests, repeated errors, false claims, sensitive disclosures, frustration, and irrelevant requests.
  7. Record versions and share only through advisor-approved settings.
When building a custom application
  • Build the smallest testable function first.
  • Separate educational decisions from technical features.
  • Use approved data and services.
  • Plan human review and failure behavior.
  • Provide a low-tech fallback.
  • Document AI-generated code, content, and revisions.

7. Test and improve

  1. Team self-test
  2. Peer-team test
  3. Teacher-client review
  4. Learner test when appropriate and approved
  5. Revised test

Record evidence, not only opinions

  • What the user tried to do
  • Where the user hesitated or misunderstood
  • What the user ignored or used successfully
  • What the user said
  • What the user actually did
  • Whether the target learning behavior changed
  • What revision follows from the evidence
Assume you may be wrong: Testing may reveal that you need to redefine the problem, change the strategy, or build a simpler product.

Team roles

RoleResponsibility
Interview and Empathy LeadConversations, follow-up questions, synthesis, permission, and privacy
Learning Research LeadTask analysis, evidence, progression, and learning myths
Design and Prototype LeadConcepts, product choice, prototypes, and versions
Testing and Evidence LeadTest questions, observation, learning evidence, and revision
Documentation and Presentation LeadDesign notebook, contributions, educational justification, and presentation

Roles should rotate or overlap. Every team member should be able to explain the whole project.

Product tracks

AI-assisted non-AI tool

AI supports research, design, or content, while the final tool is paper, physical, visual, game-based, or activity-based.

Gem or chatbot

The final tool uses conversational AI for practice, reflection, debate, question decomposition, feedback, or error analysis.

Advanced custom AI application

The team builds a custom application when added technology clearly serves the learning goal.

Technical sophistication is not educational quality. A simple paper tool can be the strongest design.

8. Prepare for the sharing event

At the sharing event, your team will present the learning problem, explain the design process, and demonstrate the learning tool you created. The goal is to help the audience understand the need, the evidence behind the design, what happened during testing, and what your team learned.

Build the project story

  1. Introduce the need: Explain the teacher-client’s challenge and the intended learner without sharing identifying information.
  2. Show what you learned from people: Share the most important empathy findings and an assumption that changed.
  3. Define the problem: Name the smallest meaningful component and briefly show the task analysis.
  4. Explain the evidence: Identify the learning-science ideas that shaped the tool and how strong or limited the evidence is.
  5. Explain the product choice: Show the alternatives you considered and why this format fit the learner action.
  6. Show testing and revision: Explain what users did, what surprised the team, and what changed in the next version.
  7. Name limitations and next steps: Be clear about what the tool has not yet been shown to do.

Plan the tool demonstration

Choose one focused scenario

Demonstrate the central learning interaction rather than every feature. Use a short example that shows what the learner must think, say, decide, practice, or revise.

Assign roles

Decide who introduces the learner need, who operates or role-plays the tool, who explains testing evidence, and who leads questions. Every member should understand the complete project.

Prepare the learner input

Use a non-identifying, age-appropriate example. For a conversational tool, prepare likely correct, partially correct, and incorrect responses.

Prepare a backup

Bring screenshots, a recording, printed materials, a storyboard, or a mock transcript in case the device, account, internet connection, or live tool fails.

Presentation and demo best practices

  • Lead with the human learning need—not the platform or technical features.
  • Keep the live demonstration short enough that the audience can follow the learning interaction.
  • Explain what the learner still has to do; do not make the tool appear to complete the work for the learner.
  • Use one visual, table, quote, prototype comparison, or observation to support each major claim.
  • Explain clearly when and how AI was used, including whether the final tool itself uses AI.
  • Use readable text, strong contrast, captions or transcripts, verbal descriptions, and a microphone when available.
  • Remove names and identifying details and use only approved links, images, recordings, and work samples.
  • Describe errors, incomplete testing, access barriers, and limitations honestly.

Practice questions from the audience

  • Why did your team choose this learning problem?
  • What did the teacher-client or learners say that changed your thinking?
  • Why is this the smallest meaningful component?
  • What learning-science evidence supports the design?
  • Why did you choose this product type instead of a simpler alternative?
  • What happened when someone used the tool?
  • What did your team revise?
  • What should the tool not be used for?
  • What would you test or improve next?
Before the event: Rehearse the presentation and demonstration with a timer, test the equipment, confirm permissions, check accessibility, prepare the backup, and make sure every team member can answer questions about the whole project.
