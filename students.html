Your League guide

You will work with a team to understand a real learning problem, research how people learn, create a learning tool, test it with users, improve it, and explain what you learned.

What you will do

Listen

Interview a teacher-client and peer learners. Follow stories and ask “Why?”, “Can you tell me more?”, and “Can you show me?”

Diagnose

Break a broad challenge into the smallest meaningful learning component.

Research

Use credible sources and AI-supported research while verifying every important claim.

Imagine

Create several different learning-tool ideas before choosing one.

Build

Start with a low-cost prototype. Your tool may be paper, physical, digital, conversational, or custom.

Test and improve

Observe what users do, record evidence, revise, and return to an earlier stage when needed.

Five team roles

For small teams, one person may hold more than one role. Rotate roles so building and presenting do not become fixed hierarchies.

Interview and Empathy Lead

Helps the team listen carefully and synthesize needs.

Learning Research Lead

Coordinates task analysis, evidence, progression, and learning myths.

Design and Prototype Lead

Organizes concept generation, product selection, prototypes, and versions.

Testing and Evidence Lead

Plans tests, observes behavior, and connects evidence to revision.

Documentation and Presentation Lead

Maintains the design notebook, contributions, rubric, and presentation.

Participation expectations

  • Complete the learning activities and team work.
  • Participate in practice, teacher-client, and peer learner interviews.
  • Protect privacy and use respectful language.
  • Build and test one shared team product.
  • Record decisions, evidence, revisions, and contributions.
  • Participate in a showcase or presentation when scheduled.
  • You may be invited to provide feedback about the League; details and permissions will be explained separately.

Participation expectations and time commitment

Core participation

Learn

Complete the five-lesson foundation on human learning, AI literacy, design thinking, evidence, safety, and accessibility.

Interview

Practice an empathy interview, interview a teacher-client, and listen to peer learners with permission.

Research

Analyze a task, locate credible sources, verify AI-supported research, and record evidence strength.

Design and build

Generate several options and create a low-resolution prototype. The product may or may not use AI.

Test and revise

Conduct staged tests, observe user behavior, identify unexpected outcomes, and improve the design.

Share

Participate in a showcase or presentation and explain the educational justification, evidence, and limitations.

Research and feedback activities

Students may be invited to complete surveys, interviews, or focus groups about their experience. Each activity should include a clear explanation of whether it is optional, what information is collected, and how privacy is protected.

  • Do not enter identifying information into AI tools.
  • Ask before recording interviews.
  • Use respectful, nonjudgmental language.
  • Allow interviewees and testers to stop.
  • Remove identifying details before sharing work.
  • Ask an advisor when a situation raises safety or privacy concerns.
Detailed roles and collaboration

Five team roles

Interview and Empathy Lead

Prepares starter questions, follows stories, protects permission and privacy, synthesizes needs, and checks that design remains grounded in people.

Learning Research Lead

Coordinates task analysis, source verification, evidence strength, learning myths, progression mapping, and design implications.

Design and Prototype Lead

Organizes ideation, compares product types, creates low-resolution prototypes, and records versions without controlling all technical work.

Testing and Evidence Lead

Defines test questions, stages tests, separates user statements from behavior, and connects observations to revisions.

Documentation and Presentation Lead

Maintains the design notebook, contribution log, rubric evidence, educational justification, and accessible presentation.

Group routine

  1. Review the current design mode and the evidence needed.
  2. Assign or rotate roles.
  3. Complete one focused task.
  4. Pause for a safety, accessibility, and learning-goal check.
  5. Receive one strength, one question, and one evidence-based critique.
  6. Record decisions, assumptions, versions, and contributions.
  7. Identify the next action and whether the team must move backward.

Inclusive participation

  • Offer oral, written, visual, asynchronous, and assisted ways to contribute.
  • Separate technical building from educational decision-making.
  • Use clear task chunks, models, checklists, and flexible pacing.
  • Rotate high-status tasks such as building and presenting.
  • Use accessible tools and low-tech alternatives.
  • Reduce scope instead of excluding a team member.
  • Critique the design, not the person.
  • Record individual and shared contributions.
Project examples

AI-assisted non-AI tools

Graph-scale card set

AI helps draft varied graph examples; students verify them and create physical cards that practice intervals and scale.

Test-question breakdown routine

A paper checklist helps learners identify action words, information, constraints, and steps.

Vocabulary memory game

AI drafts candidate examples; students curate a game using retrieval, spacing, and elaboration.

Reading-comprehension organizer

A graphic organizer helps learners identify evidence, inference, and confidence.

Gems and chatbots

Question-decomposition coach

Asks the learner to identify what is being asked, what information matters, and what step comes first.

Error-analysis coach

Compares the learner’s reasoning with an example and uses graduated hints.

Debate partner

Challenges claims, asks for evidence, and prompts reflection without writing the final response.

Reflection coach

Prompts planning, monitoring, strategy choice, confidence with evidence, and next steps.

Advanced or community-sensitive examples

Custom simulation

An application lets learners make a decision, observe a consequence, explain reasoning, and revise.

AI and endangered or Indigenous languages

Projects may explore language learning or documentation only with community leadership, consent, cultural and data governance, appropriate expertise, and clear limits. A technically possible tool may still be inappropriate.

Learning-progress mapper

A tool helps learners see prerequisites, current performance, next steps, and connections to advanced work.

Example review: What is the learner action? What evidence supports the strategy? What does AI add? What risk or access issue should be tested? Could a simpler tool work better?
Student privacy guidance

Do not enter into AI tools

  • Full names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, passwords, or account credentials
  • Student IDs, schedules, grades, disciplinary records, or unapproved work samples
  • Medical, disability, counseling, immigration, financial, or other sensitive information
  • Identifying information about another student, teacher, or family
  • Photographs, recordings, or documents without permission

Presentation and sharing guidance

Recommended project story

  1. Introduce the teacher-client need and intended learner without identifying information.
  2. Share empathy findings and what assumptions changed.
  3. Define the smallest meaningful component and show the task analysis.
  4. Explain the learning-science evidence and its strength.
  5. Show the options considered and why the selected tool format fit.
  6. Demonstrate the low-resolution and later prototype.
  7. Present testing evidence: what users said and what they actually did.
  8. Explain revisions, responsible AI, accessibility, impact, and limitations.
  9. Reflect on team learning and the next design cycle.

Presentation best practices

Lead with the human need

Start with the teacher-client and learner experience—not the platform.

Use evidence visually

Show an interview insight, task map, evidence record, prototype comparison, or observation.

Demonstrate briefly

Use one prepared interaction and a backup screenshot or transcript.

Share speaking roles

Every team member contributes and can answer questions about the whole project.

Explain productive struggle

Show what the learner must still think, decide, practice, or reflect on.

Name limitations

Describe errors, untested contexts, access barriers, and what the tool should not do.

Protect privacy

Remove names, account information, sensitive examples, and identifying media.

Design accessible materials

Use large text, strong contrast, captions, descriptive headings, and verbal descriptions.

Prepare for failure

Have a non-live demonstration and a plan if the technology is unavailable.

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the sharing stage

  • Remove identifying details and obtain permissions.
  • Do not publicly share unapproved links, recordings, or learner work.
  • Explain AI use and limitations honestly.
  • Provide captions, readable slides, language access, and alternative formats.
  • Do not overstate impact from a small number of users or tests.
  • Credit sources, collaborators, and community contributors.
