Lesson 5 · Test + Share

Try it, improve it, and tell the story

Back to lesson

Lesson purpose

Help students decide what a test can actually tell them, revise from observed evidence, and prepare an honest demonstration of what they built and learned.

Student stage: Test + Share

Materials

  • prototype
  • testing worksheet or student Test stage
  • permission/approval as needed
  • device or paper backup
  • sharing-event story organizer

Before the session

Try the activity yourself, read the short explanation, and decide what adaptations your students may need.

Advisor preparation

Try the student activity

  • Try separating “the learner liked it” from evidence that the learner’s thinking actually changed.
  • Choose one, then check what you notice.
  • Imagine a tester gets stuck because the directions are unclear. Decide whether you would revise the learning strategy or first revise the access/usability problem. Notice that the same test can reveal different kinds of problems.

Why this matters

  • Where did you want to jump to a solution?
  • What information did you realize you were missing?
  • What might a student need modeled rather than merely explained?
  • Testing should answer a focused question. Usability and learning are related but different. A useful test gives the team evidence for one meaningful revision, even when the prototype does not work as hoped.

Plan your support

  • Choose one example of weak evidence (“They liked it”) and one example of stronger evidence (“They explained it independently on a new task”). Use both when setting expectations for the test.

Student meeting: 1 × 45-minute meeting

1. Plan one focused test 0–8 minutes

What to do

  1. Name who should test this version and why that person is appropriate.
  2. Write the exact task the tester will try.
  3. Identify 2–3 things the team will observe rather than only asking for opinions.
  4. Define what would count as evidence of progress on the learning target.
  5. Write one result that would make the team reconsider the problem definition rather than simply change the interface.

Students produce: a written test plan before testing begins.

Support/challenge options:
Use when students need more support Script the test and the sharing story

Use this when: Students lead the tester too much, forget what to observe, or struggle to organize the presentation. How to run it Give students a three-part test script: introduce task, stay neutral while observing, ask two reflection questions afterward. Assign one facilitator and one observer. Use an observation checklist with boxes for hesitation, help request, successful target behavior, and access problem. After the test, complete presentation sentence starters for challenge, evidence, design, revision, and limitation. Rehearse the demonstration once with the advisor before the event. Materials: test script, observation checklist, presentation cards, demo checklist, printed backup scree

2. Run the lightest useful test 8–20 minutes

What to do

  1. Begin with a team self-test or peer test if the prototype is still rough.
  2. Move to teacher review or learner testing only when appropriate and approved.
  3. One student should facilitate while another observes when possible.
  4. Do not coach the tester through problems the team needs to observe.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Plan and run one test Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: one completed attempt that gives the team something concrete to analyze.

3. Record what actually happened 20–28 minutes

What to do

  1. Record where the tester begins, hesitates, asks for help, ignores something, changes strategy, or succeeds.
  2. Separate comments such as “I liked it” from behavioral evidence.
  3. Record whether the target learning behavior appeared and under what conditions.
  4. Note access or usability barriers that may have affected performance.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Plan and run one test Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: an observation record that distinguishes behavior, opinion, learning evidence, and access issues.

4. Revise one high-value thing 28–35 minutes

What to do

  1. Identify the observation most directly connected to the test question.
  2. Choose one revision that responds to that evidence.
  3. Write why the team expects the revision to help.
  4. In the two-session version, use a comparable task to retest. In the 45-minute version, document the planned retest if time runs out.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Plan and run one test Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: one evidence-linked revision and, when possible, a comparable retest.

5. Run a quick comparable retest 35–41 minutes

What to do

  1. Use the same core task or a closely matched version.
  2. Change only the feature you revised when possible.
  3. Record whether learner behavior changed and whether the amount of help changed.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Plan and run one test Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: a comparison between the first and revised version

Support/challenge options:
Optional extension Test transfer or compare versions

Use this when: The team completed the core test and wants stronger evidence about whether the learning support extends beyond the original example. How to run it Create a new but related task that requires the same target learning process. Predict what should transfer if the design is helping. Run the revised prototype on the new task or compare version A and B on parallel tasks. Record similarities and differences in learner behavior. Revise the team’s claim so it matches the expanded evidence. Materials: parallel task, version A/B record, simple comparison chart. You are ready to continue when: students can explain whether the observed improvement generalized and what remains uncertain.

Use when students are ready for more challenge Test an edge case and defend the limits of the claim

Use this when: The team has a stable prototype and can reason about where it may fail. How to run it Identify a learner, task, accessibility condition, or input that may challenge the design. Predict what the prototype will do and what would count as a failure. Run the edge-case or transfer test safely. Add the result to the limitations section of the sharing story. Explain which future test would be most valuable next. Materials: edge-case prompts, transfer task, accessibility test checklist, limitations matrix. You are ready to continue when: students can state not only what worked, but also the boundary of what their current evidence supports.

6. Seed the Learning Showcase story 41–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask the team to name the original challenge, one important insight, what they built, what testing changed, and one thing they learned about learning or AI.
  2. Do not build the whole presentation yet. Save these as the story spine.

Students produce: five short notes ready for the Learning Showcase Builder

Session 2 — 45 minutes

5. Retest the revised version 0–15 minutes

What to do

  1. Use the revised prototype on the same core target or a comparable new example.
  2. Keep enough conditions consistent that the team can interpret the difference.
  3. Record both usability and target learning behavior.

6. Compare results and calibrate the claim 15–25 minutes

What to do

  1. Place the two test records side by side.
  2. Name what improved, what did not, and what remains uncertain.
  3. Write the smallest claim the evidence actually supports.
  4. If the evidence contradicts the original problem, name what needs to be redefined.

7. Build the Learning Showcase story 25–35 minutes

What to do

  1. Open the Learning Showcase Builder.
  2. Have students explain the challenge, what they learned from the learner and evidence, the design, why it fits learning, what testing showed, what changed, and what remains uncertain.
  3. Require a short statement about what they learned about human learning and about AI—even if the final product is non-AI.
  4. Keep claims proportional to the evidence.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Build your story Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: a complete project story that can be rehearsed.

8. Rehearse one focused demonstration 35–42 minutes

What to do

  1. Choose one short interaction that makes the learning design visible.
  2. Assign who introduces the problem, who demonstrates, and who explains evidence/revision.
  3. Practice the demo once without interruption.
  4. Prepare screenshots, cards, or a scripted backup in case technology fails.
  5. Ask: Can an audience member understand what the learner is supposed to think or do?

Student website step: Student Step 4 · Plan the demo Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: a short, understandable demo with a backup plan.

What students should have at the end

  • A focused test plan tied to the learning objective and observable evidence of success.
  • At least one testing record describing what the learner actually did and said.
  • One evidence-linked revision—and a retest when time allows.
  • A Learning Showcase story that explains the challenge, design, testing, revision, what the team learned about learning and AI, and what remains uncertain.
  • A short demonstration plan with an accessible backup.

Help students finish the story and demonstration

  • Complete one evidence-linked revision and a comparable retest when time allows.
  • Have students finish the Learning Showcase Builder and select one short interaction to demonstrate.
  • Review privacy, accessibility, claims, permissions, and backup plans before the Learning Showcase.
