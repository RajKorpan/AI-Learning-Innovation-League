Your goal

Your goal

Students will distinguish learning from simply getting an answer, understand a few essential roles and limits of generative AI, and gather evidence about a learning experience through neutral, respectful interviewing or structured self-observation.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

Human-centered learning design starts with the person experiencing the learning challenge. Students may be designing to help themselves learn or to help someone else learn; either way, they need evidence about what the learner experiences before they decide what to build.

This lesson also establishes a second outcome that continues through the League: students are not only designing a learning aid—they are learning about how people learn and what AI can and cannot tell us about learning.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Decide whether each project begins from the student's own learning challenge or another learner's challenge.
  • Confirm who students may interview and what information should not be recorded or entered into AI tools.
  • Review the student Empathize stage and Interview Coach.
  • Choose one simple symptom-versus-breakdown example you can discuss with the group.
  • For a real teacher-client interview, confirm the teacher is available and understands that the student's job is to learn about the problem—not pitch a solution.
One meeting or two?

In a 75–90 minute block, you can introduce learning/AI, practice interviewing, and conduct a short interview. If students need to interview a teacher-client or learner outside the regular meeting, end the first meeting after interview preparation and begin the second with interview synthesis.

Core materials

Student Empathize page; Interview Coach; notebook/shared notes; sample learning task; optional sticky notes/highlighters.

Try this

Try this

Core activities are the minimum League experience. Keep these even when time is short. A lesson may span more than one meeting; do not remove evidence-gathering or testing simply to fit one session.

1

Open with the League challenge

5–8 min

Ask: “Think of something you have learned that once felt difficult. What changed?” Briefly distinguish learning from finishing a task, getting an answer, or receiving an explanation.

2

Mini module: People learn; AI generates

8–10 min

Use the two short background modules below. Students should leave with one idea about human learning and one caution about interpreting AI output.

3

Practice symptom versus breakdown

8–10 min

Give a statement such as “I’m bad at graphs” or “I forget biology vocabulary.” Ask what is observable, what is still unknown, and what you would need to ask before explaining the problem.

4

Model an empathy interview

10 min

The advisor interviews a student volunteer—or a student interviews the advisor—using one leading question and then a neutral version. Have the group identify the difference.

5

Students prepare and interview

20–30 min

Students use the Interview Coach. For another-person projects, interview a teacher-client/learner when possible. For self-learning projects, students combine structured self-observation with at least one outside perspective when possible.

6

Synthesize without solving

10–15 min

Students record what they heard or observed, what surprised them, what they may be assuming, and what they still need to know. Do not allow solution selection yet.

Just-in-time advisor learning

Open these only when you need the concept. Each is designed to take about two minutes and includes language you can immediately use with students.

2-minute background · What counts as learning

Key idea: Learning is a relatively durable change in knowledge, skill, strategy, or understanding—not merely seeing an answer or completing a task.

Say to students: “A tool can make work easier without helping you learn. We care about what changes in what the learner can understand or do.”

Quick example: A worked solution may help a student finish one problem; explaining the next problem independently is stronger evidence of learning.

Avoid: Treating speed, completion, confidence, or enjoyment by itself as proof of learning.

2-minute background · Generative AI

Key idea: Generative AI produces responses by using patterns learned from data plus the current context. Its output can be useful, fluent, incomplete, biased, or wrong.

Say to students: “AI can help us explore ideas and build things, but an AI response is not evidence about what a learner actually needs.”

Quick example: Ask an AI system for three possible reasons a student might misread a graph, then label them “hypotheses to investigate,” not conclusions.

Avoid: Saying or implying that fluent output means the system understands the learner in the same way a person does.

2-minute background · Empathy interviewing

Key idea: An empathy interview helps you understand experience before you explain it. Neutral questions invite evidence; leading questions push the person toward your theory.

Say to students: “Your job is to become more curious, not more certain.”

Quick example: Leading: “Would hints help you?” Neutral: “What do you do when you get stuck?”

Avoid: Turning the interview into a sales pitch, diagnosis, or list of solution ideas.

What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A clear statement of whether the project is intended to help the student themself or another learner.
  • Interview or self-observation notes that separate what was observed from what was inferred.
  • At least 2–3 empathy insights and one changed or challenged assumption.
  • A list of unanswered questions to investigate during Define.
  • A short reflection: What did I learn about how people learn? What did I learn about what AI can and cannot tell me?

Optional extension

Human learning versus machine output

Give students one learning episode and one AI-generated explanation. Ask: What would count as evidence that a person learned? What can we infer from the AI output—and what can we not infer?

Materials: one sample student task, one AI response, comparison sheet.

Use when students need more support

Make the interview concrete

Role-play the first interview as a group. Give students three starter questions and color-code observation, interpretation, and solution.

Materials: starter-question card, interview role cards, sample notes, highlighters or three sticky-note colors.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Compare perspectives

Have students conduct two interviews or compare self-observation with a teacher/peer perspective. Ask them to identify agreement, contradiction, and what additional evidence would resolve the difference.

Materials: student-designed interview protocol, comparison matrix, second perspective or work sample.
