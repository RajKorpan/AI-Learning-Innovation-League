Session 1 · Empathize

Help students begin with a real learner—including themselves—rather than a technology idea. Students learn a small amount about human learning and generative AI, then listen for what is actually difficult before proposing solutions.

Try AI literacy

An AI system says, “The learner may not understand rate of change.” What is that statement?

Choose one, then check what you notice.

Step 2

Why this matters

What to notice

What was difficult or tempting?

  • Where did you want to jump to a solution?
  • What information did you realize you were missing?
  • What might a student need modeled rather than merely explained?
Why it matters

The idea behind the activity

Learning is a change in knowledge, skill, strategy, or understanding that can be used again. Generative AI can suggest hypotheses or possibilities, but it cannot tell you what a particular learner needs without evidence from the learner and task.

Step 2

Know the purpose

Your goal

Your goal

Students will make something quickly, practice two neutral-question choices, conduct a real interview, and turn what they heard and observed into an Empathy Map that carries into Define.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

Empathize is not an interview-methods course. Students need only enough preparation to avoid leading the person, ask for examples, and listen for what actually happens.

The real interview is the core activity. AI practice is optional and brief. Students leave with clues and questions—not a diagnosis or a finished solution.

Step 3

Get ready

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Decide whether each project is starting from a learning challenge the student experiences personally or a challenge experienced by someone else.
  • Confirm who students may interview, whether any permissions are needed, and what personal information should not be recorded or entered into AI systems.
  • Open the student Empathize stage and skim the Interview Coach.
  • Prepare one simple example of a broad symptom such as “I’m bad at graphs” or “I can’t remember biology vocabulary.”
  • If students will interview a teacher or learner during the meeting, confirm that person’s availability. Otherwise, use the meeting for preparation and schedule the interview as soon as practical.

Core materials

Student Empathize page; a notebook or shared notes; one sample learning task; Interview Coach; optional sticky notes or highlighters.

Safety and access to plan for Empathize

  • Before interviews, confirm what participation, permission, recording, and privacy expectations apply in your setting.
  • Give students a way to participate without disclosing a personal difficulty. A student designing for themself can describe the learning task without sharing sensitive information.
  • Offer interview questions in writing and allow oral, written, visual, or assisted responses when appropriate.
  • Do not put names, grades, disability information, or other identifying learner details into an AI system.
  • If families need advance information about interviewing or participation, communicate before the activity rather than after it.

What to explain

Learning evidence + AI limits

1-minute explainer: Finishing one task is not the same as learning. AI can suggest examples and possibilities, but it cannot tell you what a real learner needs without evidence from that learner.

Try saying: “We are going to use AI as a helper, but the real person is our evidence. First we listen.”

Example: A learner may copy a worked answer correctly but still be unable to explain or use the idea on a new problem.

Watch for: Do not let students turn the quick build into their project idea.

Finish this step with: one rough build and one idea about what real learning evidence would look like
2Question Detective7–12 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Open Student Step 3. Let students choose an answer before you explain anything.
  2. Run the two short clues. After each choice, ask: “What did this question assume?”
  3. Point students to the small question bank and move on. They do not need to master interviewing first.

3Plan the interview12–20 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Open Student Step 4 and have students choose who they will interview.
  2. Students name the STEM topic/task and choose 3–5 starter questions from the bank.
  3. Have them add one question of their own only if they have a genuine question they want answered.
  4. If a student is nervous, open the optional 3-minute AI rehearsal. Otherwise skip it.

4Conduct the real interview20–35 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Students conduct the real interview with their teacher, another learner, or structured self-observation.
  2. Remind them to ask for a specific example when they hear an important claim.
  3. Keep notes short: what the person said, what they did, and what they already tried.
  4. If the interview cannot happen during the meeting, students schedule/complete it before moving fully into Define.

5Build the Empathy Map35–42 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Open Student Step 5 immediately after the interview.
  2. Students use short phrases to fill the six Empathy Map boxes: trying to do, what they do now, where it seems hard, what they tried, direct clues, and open questions.
  3. If they are unsure where it gets hard, they should write that uncertainty rather than invent an explanation.
  4. Generate the map and read it aloud once.
Student stageStudent Step 5 · Build your Empathy MapOpen the matching student step and facilitate from there.
Need more support? Use exact words first

Use this when: students start interpreting before recording evidence

  1. Copy one short phrase from the interview into “What did you hear or see?”
  2. Fill “Trying to do” from the interview topic.
  3. Leave “Where it seems hard” as “I’m not sure yet” if needed.

Materials: interview notes + Empathy Map

Ready for more? Add another perspective

Use this when: students have a clear first map and time remains

  1. Ask one second person or examine one work sample.
  2. Mark one clue that agrees and one that differs.
  3. Carry the difference into Define as an open question.

Materials: second perspective or work sample

Finish this step with: a saved Empathy Map that can carry into Define

Before the next meeting

Between meetings

Keep the project moving without adding another full session

Use only what fits your students and schedule. These tasks can be completed independently, with a teammate, or during a short facilitator check-in.

  1. Complete any learner or teacher interview that did not fit in the meeting.
  2. Have students save exact observations separately from interpretations.
  3. Ask students to bring one sample task, attempt, or example into Define when appropriate.

Next meeting: begin by asking students what they tried, what changed, and what they still need help figuring out.
