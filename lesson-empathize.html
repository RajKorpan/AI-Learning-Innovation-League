Your goal

Your goal

Students will distinguish learning from simply getting an answer, understand a few essential roles and limits of generative AI, and gather evidence about a learning experience through neutral, respectful interviewing or structured self-observation.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

Human-centered learning design starts with the person experiencing the learning challenge. Students may be designing to help themselves learn or to help someone else learn; either way, they need evidence about what the learner experiences before they decide what to build.

This lesson also establishes a second outcome that continues through the League: students are not only designing a learning aid—they are learning about how people learn and what AI can and cannot tell us about learning.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Decide whether each project is starting from a learning challenge the student experiences personally or a challenge experienced by someone else.
  • Confirm who students may interview, whether any permissions are needed, and what personal information should not be recorded or entered into AI systems.
  • Open the student Empathize stage and skim the Interview Coach.
  • Prepare one simple example of a broad symptom such as “I’m bad at graphs” or “I can’t remember biology vocabulary.”
  • If students will interview a teacher-client or learner during the meeting, confirm that person’s availability. Otherwise, plan to use the 45-minute version for preparation and schedule the interview immediately afterward.

Core materials

Student Empathize page; a notebook or shared notes; one sample learning task; Interview Coach; optional sticky notes or highlighters.

Safety and access to plan for Empathize

  • Before interviews, confirm what participation, permission, recording, and privacy expectations apply in your setting.
  • Give students a way to participate without disclosing a personal difficulty. A student designing for themself can describe the learning task without sharing sensitive information.
  • Offer interview questions in writing and allow oral, written, visual, or assisted responses when appropriate.
  • Do not put names, grades, disability information, or other identifying learner details into an AI system.
  • If families need advance information about interviewing or participation, communicate before the activity rather than after it.

1Start with a real learning experience0–5 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask every student to think of something that used to be difficult to learn.
  2. Have them name what changed: more practice, a better explanation, a strategy, feedback, prior knowledge, or something else.
  3. Write two columns where everyone can see them: finished the task and learned something. Ask students what evidence would distinguish the two.
What to explain

What counts as learning

Key idea: Learning is a change in knowledge, skill, strategy, or understanding that can be used again—not simply seeing an answer or finishing one task.

Student-friendly language: “A learning tool should change something the learner can understand or do, not just make the current task disappear.”

Example: A worked solution can help someone finish one problem. Explaining or solving a related problem later is stronger evidence of learning.

Watch for: Do not use speed, completion, confidence, or enjoyment alone as proof that learning occurred.

Finish this step with: one example of evidence that would show learning rather than task completion.
2Human learning + generative AI essentials5–10 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask students what they think generative AI is doing when it answers a question.
  2. Explain the key idea below in less than two minutes.
  3. Give one example of a useful role for AI in the League and one thing AI cannot establish by itself.
What to explain

What generative AI can and cannot tell us

Key idea: Generative AI creates responses from learned patterns and the current context. Its output can help brainstorm, explain, simulate, or build—but it can also be incomplete, biased, or wrong.

Student-friendly language: “AI can give us possibilities to investigate. It cannot tell us what this learner actually needs unless we gather evidence from the learner and the task.”

Example: Ask AI for three possible reasons someone might misread a graph. Label the output “hypotheses,” then investigate which, if any, fits.

Watch for: Do not present fluent AI output, an AI-generated citation, or an AI guess about a learner as evidence.

Open with studentsHuman learning + AI orientationStudents connect the project goal to learning and responsible AI use.
Finish this step with: one useful AI role and one AI limitation students can explain in their own words.
3Turn a symptom into questions10–17 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Put a broad statement on the board, such as “I’m bad at graphs.”
  2. Ask: What can we actually observe from this statement? What is still unknown?
  3. Generate at least three neutral questions that could reveal where the difficulty occurs.
  4. Repeat quickly with a second example or with a student’s own project challenge.
What to explain

Symptoms are not yet learning breakdowns

Key idea: Broad complaints describe an experience, not necessarily the point in the learning process that needs support.

Student-friendly language: “When someone says “I’m bad at this,” our next move is curiosity: What are they trying to do? What can they already do? When does it start getting hard?”

Example: “I forget vocabulary” could involve weak retrieval practice, unfamiliar meanings, confusion among similar terms, or an access issue.

Watch for: Avoid assigning a cause from one statement or turning a self-description into a fixed label about ability.

Finish this step with: a list of questions the team still needs to answer before it can define the problem.
4Model a neutral empathy interview17–25 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask a student volunteer to describe a familiar learning challenge, or let a student interview you.
  2. First demonstrate a leading question such as “Would hints help you?” Ask the group what the question assumes.
  3. Replace it with a neutral question such as “What do you do when you get stuck?”
  4. Model one follow-up: “Can you show me an example?” or “What happened next?”
  5. Ask students to name what made the second version more useful.
What to explain

Neutral interviewing

Key idea: An empathy interview is for understanding experience before explaining or fixing it. Neutral questions make room for evidence; leading questions push the person toward the interviewer’s theory.

Student-friendly language: “Your job is to become more curious, not more certain.”

Example: Leading: “Do you get confused because the directions are too long?” Neutral: “Walk me through what happens when you read the directions.”

Watch for: Do not turn the interview into a diagnosis, a sales pitch, or brainstorming solutions.

Open with studentsInterview CoachStudents draft neutral questions, follow-ups, and a short interview plan.
Finish this step with: a short interview protocol the student can actually use.
5Interview the learner—or observe your own learning25–37 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. For another-person projects, have the student conduct a short interview with the learner or teacher-client.
  2. For self-design projects, have the student work through a real example and narrate what they notice. Add an outside perspective when possible.
  3. Ask students to record exact observations or short paraphrases before writing interpretations.
  4. Remind students not to record unnecessary identifying information.
Open with studentsInterview notesRecord what happened, what was said, and what the student still needs to understand.
Finish this step with: notes that separate observations from the team’s interpretations.
6Synthesize without choosing a solution37–45 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask students to highlight 2–3 observations that seem especially important.
  2. For each observation, ask: What might this mean? What else could explain it?
  3. Write one assumption that changed or became less certain.
  4. End with 1–3 unanswered questions that should carry into Define.
  5. Do not let the team decide on a product yet.
Finish this step with: 2–3 empathy insights, one challenged assumption, and unanswered questions for Define.

5Prepare interview questions with Interview Coach34–45 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Open the Interview Coach with students.
  2. Have each student draft a small set of neutral starter questions.
  3. Ask partners to identify any question that assumes a cause or solution and revise it.
  4. Choose two follow-up stems students can use when they hear something important.

2Separate observations from interpretations20–30 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Draw two columns: “What we heard/saw” and “What we think it might mean.”
  2. Move each interview note into one of the two columns.
  3. Rewrite interpretations that sound certain as questions or hypotheses.
  4. Mark one point that needs more evidence.
Finish this step with: a set of observations that is clearly separated from hypotheses

4Advisor checkpoint40–45 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask the team who is experiencing the challenge and what they are trying to learn.
  2. Ask for two direct observations or statements that surprised them.
  3. Ask what the team still does not know.
  4. Do not let the team choose a product yet.
Finish this step with: an empathy summary and a clear unanswered question
What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A clear statement of whether the project is intended to help the student themself or another learner.
  • Interview or self-observation notes that separate what was observed from what was inferred.
  • At least 2–3 empathy insights and one changed or challenged assumption.
  • A list of unanswered questions to investigate during Define.
  • A short reflection: What did I learn about how people learn? What did I learn about what AI can and cannot tell me?

Need more?

Choose only the option that responds to what you are seeing. Each adaptation has a clear purpose, a short procedure, and a stopping point so it does not turn into an extra lesson.

Optional extensionCompare human learning with machine output

Use this when: Students are curious about AI or are treating AI performance as equivalent to human learning.

How to run it

  1. Give students a completed learning task and a fluent AI-generated explanation of the same topic.
  2. Ask them to list what each artifact tells us and what it cannot tell us.
  3. Have students identify what additional evidence would be needed to know whether a person learned.
  4. End by writing one rule for how the team will treat AI-generated information during the project.

Materials: one sample student task, one AI response, a two-column comparison sheet.

You are ready to continue when: students can explain why AI output and human learning evidence are different.

Use when students need more supportRehearse the interview before the real one

Use this when: Students write leading questions, freeze during interviews, or mix observations with solutions.

How to run it

  1. Give each student three starter stems: “Walk me through…,” “What happens when…,” and “Can you show me…?”
  2. Role-play a two-minute interview with the advisor as the learner.
  3. Pause after each question and label it neutral, leading, or solution-focused.
  4. Use three colors to mark observation, interpretation, and solution in the notes.
  5. Repeat the first two questions until the student can ask them without reading every word.

Materials: starter-question card, interview role cards, sample notes, three highlighter or sticky-note colors.

You are ready to continue when: students can ask at least three neutral questions and keep observations separate from proposed solutions.

Use when students are ready for more challengeCompare two perspectives

Use this when: Students have a strong first interview and can handle conflicting evidence.

How to run it

  1. Gather a second perspective: another learner, teacher-client, peer, or a work sample.
  2. Create three columns: agrees, differs, still unknown.
  3. Identify one contradiction that matters for the project.
  4. Design one additional question or observation that could help resolve the contradiction.
  5. Carry both explanations into Define rather than forcing agreement.

Materials: comparison matrix, second interview/work sample, student-designed follow-up question.

You are ready to continue when: students can explain where evidence agrees, where it conflicts, and what they would investigate next.
