Your goal

Your goal

Students will map the learning task in sequence, identify what the learner can already do, locate a likely point of breakdown, distinguish evidence from assumption, and finish with a focused problem statement small enough to design and test.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

A broad statement such as “I struggle with algebra” or “students do not understand graphs” does not tell a design team what to change. Define asks students to rewind the task until they can see where performance changes.

This is also where students begin connecting their observations to learning science. The goal is not to diagnose a person; it is to develop a testable explanation of a learning breakdown that can be revised when new evidence appears.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Review the Empathize artifacts and confirm that the team has actual observations, not only a solution idea.
  • Choose a privacy-safe example task you can decompose together into 4–6 steps.
  • Review the student Learning Breakdown Mapper and Claim & Evidence Tracker.
  • Identify likely disciplinary prerequisites or representations you may need to help students notice without giving them the answer.
  • Prepare a reminder that AI-generated explanations and citations must be verified against original evidence.

Core materials

Student Define page; privacy-safe sample work or task; Learning Breakdown Mapper; Claim & Evidence Tracker; notebook/shared notes.

Try this

Try this

Core activities are the minimum League experience. Keep these even when time is short. A lesson may span more than one meeting; do not remove evidence-gathering or testing simply to fit one session.

1

Return to empathy evidence

5–8 min

Ask the team to name the learning situation, what the learner is trying to do, and two pieces of evidence from Empathize. Do not begin with the planned tool.

2

Model a task map

10–12 min

Take one sample task and map what happens first, what happens next, what knowledge is needed, and where a learner could reasonably get stuck.

3

Build the Learning Breakdown Map

25–30 min

Students map the overall task, steps, knowledge/representations, points of success, confusion, likely bottleneck, and evidence. Encourage them to revise earlier steps as they notice prerequisites.

4

Check access versus understanding

5–8 min

Ask whether the difficulty could be caused or amplified by language, directions, visual presentation, motor demands, device access, or cognitive load rather than the target concept itself.

5

Turn explanations into claims

10–15 min

Students use the Claim & Evidence Tracker: What do we think is true? What supports it? How strong is that evidence? What does it mean for the design?

6

Approve a focused problem statement

10 min

Use the mapper’s readiness classification and advisor discussion to decide whether the problem is too broad, almost there, or ready to design. If evidence is weak, send the team back to gather it rather than forcing a definition.

Just-in-time advisor learning

Open these only when you need the concept. Each is designed to take about two minutes and includes language you can immediately use with students.

2-minute background · Task decomposition

Key idea: Complex performance depends on smaller knowledge, decisions, representations, and actions. The final wrong answer may occur after the real breakdown began.

Say to students: “We are going to rewind the task until we find the first step that changes.”

Quick example: A student gets slope wrong. The issue might be the formula—or earlier, identifying rise/run, reading axis intervals, or interpreting negative direction.

Avoid: Treating the last visible error as automatically being the cause.

2-minute background · Evidence versus assumption

Key idea: A plausible explanation is not yet evidence. Good design decisions connect claims to observations, work samples, credible sources, or tests.

Say to students: “Write what you think is happening, then ask what would convince someone else—and what could prove you wrong.”

Quick example: Claim: “The student forgets vocabulary because they only reread.” Evidence: interview + study routine + later-recall performance.

Avoid: Using an AI-generated statement or citation as evidence without checking the original source.

2-minute background · Access or learning?

Key idea: Sometimes performance is limited by how a task is presented or accessed rather than by the underlying knowledge itself.

Say to students: “Before we design instruction, ask whether the learner can actually access the instructions, representation, language, and interaction.”

Quick example: A student may understand a graph concept but struggle because labels are small, the interface requires precise dragging, or directions are linguistically complex.

Avoid: Interpreting an access barrier as a deficit in ability or motivation.

What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A task map showing the overall task, meaningful intermediate steps, needed knowledge/representations, and where the learner succeeds or becomes confused.
  • A synthesis that can complete: The learner can already… The breakdown seems to happen when… Our evidence is… Therefore our project will focus on…
  • At least one verified evidence/claim record connected to a design implication.
  • A focused problem statement that is small enough for one prototype and includes observable evidence of improvement.
  • A reflection: What did mapping the task teach us about learning? Did AI help us generate hypotheses, and how did we verify them?

Need more?

Optional extension

Explore one relevant learning-science concept

Only after the task is mapped, examine a concept that might explain the breakdown—such as prior knowledge, working memory demands, representation, retrieval, feedback, or metacognitive monitoring.

Materials: one short credible source, Claim & Evidence Tracker.

Use when students need more support

Build the first map physically

Write each step of a familiar task on a card. Let students arrange, split, combine, and annotate the cards before entering the sequence online.

Materials: step cards or sticky notes, worked example, “can do / gets stuck / not sure” markers, sentence starters.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Test competing explanations

Have the team create two plausible bottleneck explanations and identify what evidence would distinguish them. Ask them to revise the map after reviewing a second task or work sample.

Materials: second sample, comparison table, additional source or interview evidence.
