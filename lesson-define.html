Facilitation Guide

Mission

Help students turn interview clues into one small, testable STEM learning problem and a clear learning goal.

Key questionWhere does it first get hard, and what smaller part can we actually design for?
Use this guide when you need it

Prepare, then facilitate

Before the sessionStep 1
Step 1

Prepare

You do not need to reteach the student activity here. Prepare the conditions that let students do the thinking themselves.

Before students arrive

  • Have the students’ Empathy Map available.
  • Preview the built-in Task Breakdown Helper so you can point students to it if they get stuck.
  • Have a worked example ready that shows a big task broken into smaller steps.
  • Plan how students can represent steps physically or on paper if typing is difficult.
Step 2

Know what to watch for

Watch for

  • Broad statements such as “bad at math” or “doesn’t understand science.”
  • Students treating a guess about the learner as a diagnosis.
  • Skipping the steps immediately before the difficulty.
  • Choosing a product before the learning goal is clear.

If students get stuck, try saying

  • “What happens immediately before it gets hard?”
  • “Can the learner do that smaller step by itself?”
  • “What did you actually see or hear that makes you think this?”
  • “What would the learner be able to do differently if this improved?”
Step 3

Preview the student path

The student pathway contains the actual activity, examples, tools, and response scaffolds. Open it before the session so you know what students will see.

Step 2

Activities

Use these prompts to facilitate. The actual student instructions and tools stay on the student page.

0–5

Reconnect to the Empathy Map

Ask students to name one strong clue and one unresolved question from Empathize.

Open the student activity ↗

Students produce: one evidence-based starting point

5–10

Quick challenge

Use the short case to reinforce that a useful learning problem is smaller than a broad complaint.

Open the student activity ↗

Students produce: a shared example of useful problem size

10–30

Break down the task

Students map the task one step at a time. Point them to the built-in helper if they cannot identify where the process changes.

Open the student activity ↗

Students produce: a Task Map with a draft best guess about the breakdown

30–38

Write the learning goal

Students state what the learner should be able to do and what success would look like.

Open the student activity ↗

Students produce: a focused learning goal and success indicator

38–45

Check together

Review scope, STEM connection, direct evidence, and uncertainty. Send students back if the project still tries to solve several different problems at once.

Students produce: a facilitator-checked focused problem

Step 3

What students should produce

Before moving on, students should have these artifacts or decisions. They do not need polished final work.

Session outputs

  • Task Map
  • What the learner can already do
  • Where the task first seems to change
  • Draft best guess to test
  • Learning goal
  • What success will look like
Step 4

Quick check

Use these questions to decide whether students are ready to move forward or need one more conversation/revision.

Check before moving on

  • Is the problem small enough for one prototype?
  • Can students point to direct evidence for the focus?
  • Is the language a best guess to test rather than a diagnosis?
  • Is the learning goal about what the learner will do—not what the product will contain?
