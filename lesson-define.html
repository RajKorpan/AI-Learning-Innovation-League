Your goal

Your goal

Students will map the learning task in sequence, identify what the learner can already do, locate a likely point of breakdown, distinguish evidence from assumption, and finish with a focused problem statement small enough to design and test.

Keep two outcomes visible.

Students are designing something that may help themselves or someone else learn. At the same time, they should be able to explain what this stage taught them about human learning and about the useful roles and limits of AI.

Why it matters

Why it matters

A broad statement such as “I struggle with algebra” or “students do not understand graphs” does not tell a design team what to change. Define asks students to rewind the task until they can see where performance changes.

This is also where students begin connecting their observations to learning science. The goal is not to diagnose a person; it is to develop a testable explanation of a learning breakdown that can be revised when new evidence appears.

Before you meet

Before you meet

  • Review the team’s Empathize artifacts and confirm that they contain observations, not only a product idea.
  • Choose one privacy-safe learning task you can decompose into several meaningful steps in front of students.
  • Open the student Learning Breakdown Mapper and Claim & Evidence Tracker.
  • Think about likely prerequisites, representations, or access barriers you may need to help students notice without choosing the bottleneck for them.
  • Prepare one credible learning-science source or example claim so you can model the difference between a plausible idea and evidence.

Core materials

Empathy notes; privacy-safe sample task/work; Student Define page; Learning Breakdown Mapper; Claim & Evidence Tracker; paper or digital notes.

Core activity guide

How to run each step

Open the details as you reach each activity. Explanations and student tools are placed at the moment you need them.

1Begin with evidence—not the planned tool5 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask the team to state the learning situation in one sentence.
  2. Require two pieces of evidence from Empathize: something observed, said, or shown in work.
  3. Ask the learner goal: What is the person trying to understand or do?
  4. Temporarily set aside any product idea that is already on the table.
Finish this step with: a learner goal plus two pieces of empathy evidence.
2Map the task in sequence10–20 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Model a familiar task in 4–6 steps. Say what the learner does at each step and what knowledge or representation is needed.
  2. Ask students to map their own task from the beginning rather than starting with the final error.
  3. Have them mark where the learner succeeds independently, succeeds with help, becomes inconsistent, or gets stuck.
  4. Encourage revision of earlier steps if students discover a missing prerequisite.
What to explain

Task decomposition

Key idea: Complex performance depends on smaller knowledge, decisions, representations, and actions. The last wrong answer may occur after the real breakdown began.

Student-friendly language: “We are going to rewind the task until we find the first step that changes.”

Example: A student gets slope wrong. The issue may be the formula—or earlier, reading axis intervals, identifying rise/run, or interpreting direction.

Watch for: Do not automatically treat the final visible error as the cause.

Open with studentsLearning Breakdown MapperMap the task, existing success, likely breakdown, prerequisites, evidence, and scope.
Finish this step with: a task sequence that shows where performance changes.
3Name the first likely learning breakdown8–12 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask: What can the learner already do without help?
  2. Find the earliest step where the evidence changes from success to confusion, guessing, or dependence on help.
  3. Write at least one alternative explanation for the same observation.
  4. Ask what evidence would distinguish the explanations.
Finish this step with: one leading bottleneck hypothesis and at least one alternative explanation.
4Check access before calling it a learning problem5–8 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Look at the directions, language, visual layout, device demands, timing, and interaction format.
  2. Ask whether any of these could be preventing the learner from showing what they know.
  3. If access is contributing, decide whether the design should remove the barrier, support the learning process, or both.
What to explain

Access versus understanding

Key idea: Performance can be limited by how information or interaction is presented rather than by the target knowledge itself.

Student-friendly language: “Before we teach something new, make sure the learner can access the instructions, representation, language, and interaction.”

Example: A learner may understand a graph concept but struggle because labels are tiny or directions are linguistically dense.

Watch for: Do not interpret an access barrier as low ability, low motivation, or a learning deficit.

Finish this step with: a note about access factors that must be addressed or ruled out.
5Turn explanations into claims and evidence8–12 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Ask the team to state one claim about the breakdown or about a learning strategy that may matter.
  2. Identify what currently supports the claim: interview, observation, work sample, credible source, or test.
  3. Rate how strong that evidence is and record what is still uncertain.
  4. Connect the evidence to one design implication—without deciding the product yet.
What to explain

Evidence versus assumption

Key idea: A reasonable explanation is still a hypothesis until it is connected to observations, work, credible sources, or testing.

Student-friendly language: “Write what you think is happening, then ask what would convince someone else—and what could prove you wrong.”

Example: Claim: “Rereading is not producing later recall.” Evidence: study routine + interview + later recall attempt.

Watch for: An AI-generated statement or citation is not evidence until the original source or direct evidence is verified.

Open with studentsClaim & Evidence TrackerConnect what the team thinks is true to evidence and a design implication.
Finish this step with: at least one claim/evidence record the team can defend.
6Check the scope and write the focused problem7–10 minutesDetails

How to do it

  1. Run the mapper’s readiness check.
  2. If the result is Too broad, identify which part of the task or learner group can be narrowed.
  3. If it is Almost there, make the targeted revisions before proceeding.
  4. If it is Ready to design, complete: “The learner can already… The breakdown seems to happen when… Our evidence is… Therefore our project will focus on…”
  5. Name one observable change that would count as improvement.

What students should have when they finish

What students should have when they finish

  • A task map showing the overall task, meaningful intermediate steps, needed knowledge/representations, and where the learner succeeds or becomes confused.
  • A synthesis that can complete: The learner can already… The breakdown seems to happen when… Our evidence is… Therefore our project will focus on…
  • At least one verified evidence/claim record connected to a design implication.
  • A focused problem statement that is small enough for one prototype and includes observable evidence of improvement.
  • A reflection: What did mapping the task teach us about learning? Did AI help us generate hypotheses, and how did we verify them?

Need more?

Need more?

Choose only the option that responds to what you are seeing. Each adaptation has a clear purpose, a short procedure, and a stopping point so it does not turn into an extra lesson.

Optional extension

Compare two bottleneck hypotheses

Use this when: The team has good evidence but two explanations still fit.

How to run it

  1. Write both hypotheses in parallel.
  2. For each, list evidence that supports it and evidence that would weaken it.
  3. Design one quick observation, work-sample check, or question that could distinguish them.
  4. Collect the new evidence if feasible, then revise the Task Map.
  5. Keep both explanations if the evidence remains ambiguous.

Materials: two-column hypothesis sheet, existing empathy evidence, one additional task or work sample.

You are ready to continue when: students can explain why one hypothesis is currently stronger—or why the evidence is still inconclusive.

Use when students need more support

Build the task map physically

Use this when: Students struggle to break a complex task into steps or write long explanations.

How to run it

  1. Write each task step on a separate index card.
  2. Ask students to arrange the cards from first to last and add missing steps.
  3. Under each card, place a green note for “can do,” yellow for “sometimes/with help,” or red for “breakdown.”
  4. Add a second small note naming the knowledge or representation needed at the first yellow/red step.
  5. Transfer the resulting sequence into the Learning Breakdown Mapper.

Materials: index cards, three colors of sticky notes, one worked example, marker.

You are ready to continue when: the team can point to the first meaningful change in performance and describe what may be needed there.

Use when students are ready for more challenge

Map a branching or non-linear task

Use this when: The team’s task involves decisions rather than one simple sequence.

How to run it

  1. Identify the major decision points in the task.
  2. Draw at least two branches showing what happens after different learner choices.
  3. Mark which branch is supported by current evidence and which is hypothetical.
  4. Find the earliest decision where novice and proficient performance differ.
  5. Use a credible source or additional work sample to test one assumption about that decision.

Materials: large paper/whiteboard, branch arrows, evidence notes, optional disciplinary source.

You are ready to continue when: students can explain how different pathways through the task change their bottleneck hypothesis.
