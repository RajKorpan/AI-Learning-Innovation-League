Session 3 · Ideate

Choose a way to help

Back to session

Session purpose

Help students connect the learning breakdown to plausible strategies and invent several ways to help before they choose a product or technology.

Student stage: Ideate

Materials

  • student Define outputs
  • Strategy Explorer
  • Resource Inventor
  • index cards or sticky notes
  • classroom materials students can inventory

Before the session

Try the activity yourself, read the short explanation, and decide what adaptations your students may need.

Facilitator preparation

Try the student activity

  • Try matching a learning problem to what the learner needs to do differently, then use ordinary resources as design constraints.
  • Choose one, then check what you notice.
  • Look around you and name three ordinary resources—paper, a whiteboard, a partner, objects, a hallway, a calculator. Invent one way those resources could make the learner perform the target thinking.

Why this matters

  • Where did you want to jump to a solution?
  • What information did you realize you were missing?
  • What might a student need modeled rather than merely explained?
  • A learning strategy is not a product. It describes the kind of thinking or practice that may help. AI can broaden brainstorming, but students should compare multiple ideas and keep control of the design decision.

Plan your support

  • Choose one learning challenge and one strategy example you can model. Identify at least three low-tech resources students could use if they start defaulting to a chatbot or app.

Student meeting: 1 × 45-minute meeting

1. Learn the challenge types 0–5 minutes

What to do

  1. Read the focused problem statement aloud.
  2. Ask: What do we want the learner to do differently—recall, explain, compare, plan, interpret, practice, monitor, or something else?
  3. Write that learner action where the team can see it throughout Ideate.
  4. Challenge any product-first statement such as “we want to make a chatbot” by asking what learning action the chatbot would support.

Students produce: one clear learner action the team is trying to support.

Support/challenge options:
Use when students need more support Use strategy cards and resource picture cards

Use this when: Students repeat the same kind of idea or have difficulty imagining non-digital options. How to run it Give the team 3–4 strategy cards selected from their top Strategy Explorer results. Spread resource cards or real objects on the table. Have the student pick one strategy card and two resource cards at random. Ask: “What could the learner do with these?” Sketch for two minutes. Repeat three times, then choose which idea best fits the focused problem. Materials: printed strategy cards, resource picture cards or real classroom objects, blank paper, timer. You are ready to continue when: the team has at least three genuinely different interactions, including a non-AI option.

2. Explore learning strategies step by step 5–15 minutes

What to do

  1. Before students classify their own project, open the “Which challenge fits best?” practice cases. Have them explain why the category describes the learner action rather than labeling the learner. After the Strategy Explorer produces options, have students complete the two-minute “Try the strategy” activity before deciding what they want to design.
  2. Have students enter the breakdown, learner stage, desired mental action, feedback need, evidence strength, and constraints.
  3. Read the top three Strategy Explorer results rather than accepting only the first.
  4. For each strategy, ask students to explain why it fits—or why it may not fit—the Task Map.
  5. Choose one leading strategy and one alternate to carry forward.

Students produce: a primary strategy plus at least one plausible alternative. Students should also be able to state what the learner would actually do, what the learning tool should do, and what evidence would show that the strategy helped.

3. Try the strategy for two minutes 15–20 minutes

What to do

  1. Open the two-minute Learning Lab after students choose a leading strategy hypothesis.
  2. Have students do the tiny learning task before you explain the strategy.
  3. Ask what the activity required them to remember, explain, compare, notice, or monitor.
  4. Use the result to decide whether the strategy still seems plausible for the project.

Student website step: Student Step 5 · Try the strategy Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: one sentence explaining what the strategy makes the learner do and what evidence would show it helped.

4. Turn available resources into an AI activity-design prompt 15–25 minutes

What to do

  1. Give students two minutes to physically scan the room and list resources they can realistically use: materials, devices, people, spaces, and time.
  2. Open the Resource Inventor. Students move through one question at a time: exact learner action, learning strategy, available resources, grouping, activity length, and access requirements.
  3. Have students read the generated prompt before using it. Ask: Does this tell AI to use our real constraints? Does it preserve the learner's thinking? Does it ask for several possibilities rather than one “best” answer?
  4. Paste the prompt into an facilitator-approved AI system when available. Save several useful possibilities or ingredients, but do not select one yet.
  5. If an approved AI system is not available, use the prompt as a team brainstorming challenge: each teammate invents one response to the same constraints.
  6. Add one or two learner interests only when they can make the activity more meaningful without distracting from the learning goal.
  7. Ask the AI for multiple genuinely different possibilities. Students must choose, combine, revise, or reject the ideas rather than treating the first output as the design.

Student website step: Student Step 6 · Use what you already have Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: one reviewed AI activity-design prompt and several possibilities or ingredients to critique during the Five-Idea Sprint.

5. Run the Five-Idea Sprint 25–37 minutes

What to do

  1. Set a visible timer. The goal is variety, not polish.
  2. Require one idea with no AI, one using existing classroom resources, one social/people-based idea, one AI-supported idea, and one wild-card idea.
  3. Students should sketch or describe the learner interaction—not just name a product.
  4. After all five exist, allow students to combine the strongest pieces.

Student website step: Student Step 7 · Make several ideas Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: at least five distinct concepts that deliver the learning strategy in different ways.

Support/challenge options:
Optional extension Remix two strategies and two resources

Use this when: Students have a strong initial shortlist and need more creative range. How to run it Select two different learning strategies from Strategy Explorer. Select two available resources that the team did not use in its first ideas. Generate one concept for each strategy/resource combination. Compare which combination changes the learner’s thinking in the most useful way. Add only the strongest new concept to the shortlist. Materials: strategy results, resource inventory, four-box remix sheet or sticky notes. You are ready to continue when: students can explain how changing the strategy or resource changes the learning interaction.

Use when students are ready for more challenge Design under deliberate constraints

Use this when: Students quickly generate plausible ideas and are ready to test their design assumptions. How to run it Choose one constraint such as no screens, two minutes per interaction, multilingual directions, or use by two learners together. Redesign the leading concept under that constraint. Choose a second, very different constraint and redesign again. Compare what each constraint reveals about the essential learning interaction. Decide whether any constrained version is actually stronger than the original. Materials: constraint cards, idea sketches, comparison matrix. You are ready to continue when: students can identify which parts of the idea are essential and which were merely co

6. Compare the strongest possibilities 37–43 minutes

What to do

  1. Compare the ideas against the target learner action.
  2. Ask which ideas preserve the thinking the learner needs to practice.
  3. Check accessibility, available resources, time, testability, and whether AI adds a necessary capability.
  4. Keep 2–3 ideas for Prototype instead of prematurely selecting one polished solution.

Student website step: Student Step 7 · Make several ideas Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: a shortlist of 2–3 ideas with a sentence explaining why each might help learning.

7. Facilitator checkpoint 43–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask which idea best targets the learner action and why.
  2. Ask what the team would learn by prototyping each finalist.
  3. Require at least one non-AI or low-tech possibility in the comparison.

Students produce: two ideas worth carrying into Prototype

4. Run a resource hunt 20–30 minutes

What to do

  1. Have teams physically scan the room and list materials, devices, people, and spaces they could actually use.
  2. Push beyond technology: paper, cards, rulers, whiteboards, stairs, partners, classroom routines, notes, and ordinary objects all count.
  3. Ask which resources create a useful learner action—not just decoration.
  4. Add one or two learner interests only when they can make the activity more meaningful without distracting from the learning goal.
  5. Name real constraints—time, reading load, group size, technology, pacing, and accessibility—before generating ideas.
  6. Ask the AI for multiple genuinely different possibilities. Students must choose, combine, revise, or reject the ideas rather than treating the first output as the design.

Students produce: a realistic inventory of materials, people, spaces, and devices

5. Build an AI prompt from the resources 30–40 minutes

What to do

  1. Open Resource Inventor.
  2. Move through one question at a time: learning target, strategy, materials, learner interests, participants, time, constraints, and access needs. The material and constraint choices appear as visual cards so students can scan what is actually around them.
  3. Generate the final prompt. Read it before submitting it anywhere.
  4. Ask whether the prompt tells AI to generate several possibilities and preserve the learner’s thinking.
  5. Add one or two learner interests only when they can make the activity more meaningful without distracting from the learning goal.
  6. Name real constraints—time, reading load, group size, technology, pacing, and accessibility—before generating ideas.
  7. Ask the AI for multiple genuinely different possibilities. Students must choose, combine, revise, or reject the ideas rather than treating the first output as the design.

Students produce: a reviewed prompt ready to paste into an approved AI system

6. Submit the prompt or simulate the response 40–45 minutes

What to do

  1. If an approved AI system is available, paste the prompt and save the resulting ideas.
  2. If it is not available, have teammates each imagine one response to the same prompt.
  3. Do not choose an idea yet.

Students produce: several AI- or team-generated activity possibilities

Session 2 — 45 minutes

7. Critique the generated possibilities 0–10 minutes

What to do

  1. For each possibility, ask what the learner actually does.
  2. Cross out anything that gives away the target thinking or ignores the real resources.
  3. Mark one element worth remixing from each promising idea.

9. Remix and refine 25–35 minutes

What to do

  1. Combine one useful strategy with one resource or interaction from a different idea.
  2. Change the format so at least one idea is physical/social and one may use AI only where helpful.
  3. Give each idea a one-sentence description of what the learner would actually do.

Students produce: three distinct, understandable candidate ideas

10. Shortlist two finalists 35–42 minutes

What to do

  1. Compare the ideas against the target learner action.
  2. Ask which ideas preserve the thinking the learner needs to practice.
  3. Check accessibility, available resources, time, testability, and whether AI adds a necessary capability.

11. Facilitator checkpoint 42–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask students to defend each finalist in terms of learning action, access, available resources, and testability.
  2. Check that technology is a design choice rather than the project goal.
  3. Approve two ideas to compare during Prototype.

Student website step: Student Step 8 · Check together Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: two finalists and a reason for carrying each forward

What students should have at the end

  • A selected learning-strategy hypothesis and a plain-language explanation of what the learner should actually do.
  • A resource inventory that includes materials, people, spaces, technology, constraints, and access needs.
  • Several meaningfully different design ideas, including at least one non-AI or off-screen possibility.
  • One or two finalist ideas with a reason each is worth prototyping.

Keep the project moving without adding another full session

  • Have students complete the Five-Idea Sprint if the team generated fewer than three genuinely different possibilities.
  • Encourage one quick no-tech or low-tech trial before students commit to a build.
  • Bring the top strategy, available resources, and two candidate ideas into Prototype.
  • If students actually try one Resource Inventor activity between meetings, have them complete the Resource Inventor reflection: what happened, what the learner noticed or explained, where confusion remained, and what should change.
