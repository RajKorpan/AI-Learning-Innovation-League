Session 1 · Empathize

Understand the learner

Back to session

Session purpose

Help students begin with a real learner—including themselves—rather than a technology idea. Students learn a small amount about human learning and generative AI, then listen for what is actually difficult before proposing solutions.

Student stage: Empathize

Materials

  • student devices or printed interview worksheet
  • paper or sticky notes
  • index cards for quick build
  • timer
  • approved AI tool only for optional practice

Before the session

Try the activity yourself, read the short explanation, and decide what adaptations your students may need.

Facilitator preparation

Try the student activity

  • Try two short decisions your students will face: separating learning from task completion and asking neutral questions before jumping to a solution.
  • Choose one, then check what you notice.
  • Think of something you learned recently. Name one piece of evidence that you could use the knowledge or skill again—not merely that you finished the task.
  • Choose one, then check what you notice.

Why this matters

  • Where did you want to jump to a solution?
  • What information did you realize you were missing?
  • What might a student need modeled rather than merely explained?
  • Learning is a change in knowledge, skill, strategy, or understanding that can be used again. Generative AI can suggest hypotheses or possibilities, but it cannot tell you what a particular learner needs without evidence from the learner and task.

Plan your support

  • Choose one broad learning complaint you can use as an example. Decide how you will model a neutral follow-up question and how students can participate without disclosing sensitive information.

Student meeting: 1 × 45-minute meeting

1. Quick build + connect it to learning 0–5 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask every student to think of something that used to be difficult to learn.
  2. Have them name what changed: more practice, a better explanation, a strategy, feedback, prior knowledge, or something else.
  3. Write two columns where everyone can see them: finished the task and learned something . Ask students what evidence would distinguish the two.

Students produce: one example of evidence that would show learning rather than task completion.

2. Question Detective: turn a symptom into useful questions 10–17 minutes

What to do

  1. Put a broad statement on the board, such as “I’m bad at graphs.”
  2. Ask: What can we actually observe from this statement? What is still unknown?
  3. Generate at least three neutral questions that could reveal where the difficulty occurs.
  4. Repeat quickly with a second example or with a student’s own project challenge.

Student website step: Student Step 3 · Question Detective Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: a list of questions the team still needs to answer before it can define the problem.

3. Human learning + generative AI essentials 5–10 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask students what they think generative AI is doing when it answers a question.
  2. Explain the key idea below in less than two minutes.
  3. Give one example of a useful role for AI in the League and one thing AI cannot establish by itself.

Student website step: Student Step 4 · Learn the basics Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: one useful AI role and one AI limitation students can explain in their own words.

Support/challenge options:
Optional extension Compare human learning with machine output

Use this when: Students are curious about AI or are treating AI performance as equivalent to human learning. How to run it Give students a completed learning task and a fluent AI-generated explanation of the same topic. Ask them to list what each artifact tells us and what it cannot tell us. Have students identify what additional evidence would be needed to know whether a person learned. End by writing one rule for how the team will treat AI-generated information during the project. Materials: one sample student task, one AI response, a two-column comparison sheet. You are ready to continue when: students can explain why AI output and human learning evidence are different.

4. Model a neutral empathy interview 17–25 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask a student volunteer to describe a familiar learning challenge, or let a student interview you.
  2. First demonstrate a leading question such as “Would hints help you?” Ask the group what the question assumes.
  3. Replace it with a neutral question such as “What do you do when you get stuck?”
  4. Model one follow-up: “Can you show me an example?” or “What happened next?”
  5. Ask students to name what made the second version more useful.

Students produce: a short interview protocol the student can actually use.

Use when students need more support Rehearse the interview before the real one

Repeat the first two questions until the student can ask them without reading every word. 5. Interview the learner—or observe your own learning 25–37 minutes

What to do

  1. For another-person projects, have the student conduct a short interview with the learner or a teacher who can describe the learning challenge.
  2. For self-design projects, have the student work through a real example and narrate what they notice. Add an outside perspective when possible.
  3. Ask students to record exact observations or short paraphrases before writing interpretations.
  4. Remind students not to record unnecessary identifying information.

Student website step: Student Step 5 · Plan, practice, and conduct the interview Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: notes that separate observations from the team’s interpretations.

6. Synthesize without choosing a solution 37–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask students to highlight 2–3 observations that seem especially important.
  2. For each observation, ask: What might this mean? What else could explain it?
  3. Write one assumption that changed or became less certain.
  4. End with 1–3 unanswered questions that should carry into Define.
  5. Do not let the team decide on a product yet.

Student website step: Student Step 5 · Plan, practice, and conduct the interview Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: 2–3 empathy insights, one challenged assumption, and unanswered questions for Define.

Support/challenge options:
Use when students are ready for more challenge Compare two perspectives

Use this when: Students have a strong first interview and can handle conflicting evidence. How to run it Gather a second perspective: another learner, teacher, peer, or a work sample. Create three columns: agrees, differs, still unknown. Identify one contradiction that matters for the project. Design one additional question or observation that could help resolve the contradiction. Carry both explanations into Define rather than forcing agreement. Materials: comparison matrix, second interview/work sample, student-designed follow-up question. You are ready to continue when: students can explain where evidence agrees, where it conflicts, and what they would investigate next.

5. Prepare interview questions with Interview Coach 34–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Open the Interview Coach with students.
  2. Have each student draft a small set of neutral starter questions.
  3. Ask partners to identify any question that assumes a cause or solution and revise it.
  4. Choose two follow-up stems students can use when they hear something important.

7. Separate observations from interpretations 20–30 minutes

What to do

  1. Draw two columns: “What we heard/saw” and “What we think it might mean.”
  2. Move each interview note into one of the two columns.
  3. Rewrite interpretations that sound certain as questions or hypotheses.
  4. Mark one point that needs more evidence.

Students produce: a set of observations that is clearly separated from hypotheses

9. Facilitator checkpoint 40–45 minutes

What to do

  1. Ask the team who is experiencing the challenge and what they are trying to learn.
  2. Ask for two direct observations or statements that surprised them.
  3. Ask what the team still does not know.
  4. Do not let the team choose a product yet.

Student website step: Student Step 6 · Check together Open the matching student step and facilitate from there.

Students produce: an empathy summary and a clear unanswered question

What students should have at the end

  • An interview plan with open, neutral starter questions.
  • Notes from the real interview or structured self-observation, with observations separated from interpretations.
  • Empathy clues: repeated patterns, something observed directly, one assumption that changed, and open questions to carry into Define.
  • No final solution selected yet.

Keep the project moving without adding another full session

  • Complete any learner or teacher interview that did not fit in the meeting.
  • Have students save exact observations separately from interpretations.
  • Ask students to bring one sample task, attempt, or example into Define when appropriate.
