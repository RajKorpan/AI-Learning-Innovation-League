Start and Run a Club

Plan the launch, choose the right format, and run the League over six weeks, ten weeks, or a semester.

This page combines startup planning, school-readiness checks, implementation models, and pacing options so advisors can move from initial approval to a complete student project cycle.

Step 1

Complete a readiness review

People

  • Identify an advisor or coaching team
  • Choose the grade range
  • Decide whether students work individually, in pairs, or in teams
  • Identify possible school, family, or community partners

Time and setting

  • Select in-person, virtual, or hybrid delivery
  • Choose meeting frequency and length
  • Reserve rooms or online spaces
  • Choose a six-week, ten-week, or semester model

Technology and policy

  • Confirm device and internet access
  • Review school AI and acceptable-use policies
  • Check account and age requirements
  • Prepare a paper or storyboard alternative

Safety and accessibility

  • Review privacy, accuracy, bias, and accessibility
  • Confirm how user testing will be approved
  • Plan supports for varied reading, language, and technology needs
  • Identify when an adult must intervene

Students and families

  • Recruit students
  • Share a plain-language family overview
  • Collect required permissions
  • Explain what students will create and how work may be shared

Evidence and improvement

  • Decide what student artifacts will be saved
  • Plan feedback from students, advisors, and families
  • Choose a showcase format
  • Schedule a final reflection and revision meeting
Step 2

Choose an implementation model

One-on-one tutoring

A teacher guides one student through the lessons and design process. The student may use self-reflection, family interviews, peer feedback, or virtual testing to bring in another perspective.

Best practices: use shorter discussions, record the student’s reasoning, avoid over-directing the project, and add at least one outside user or reviewer before revision.

Small-group club

Students interview one another, work in pairs or teams, rotate roles, test prototypes with peers, and prepare a shared presentation.

Best practices: assign roles, use contribution logs, schedule cross-team critiques, and make sure every student can explain the learning rationale.

Classroom integration

The five lessons are taught during class, followed by project work during class, independent work time, or club meetings.

Best practices: connect examples to current STEM content, use common checkpoints, and allow multiple prototype formats.

Virtual club

Students from one or more schools meet online for lessons, interviews, design reviews, tool testing, and presentations.

Best practices: use clear agendas, shared documents, breakout groups, assigned facilitators, and privacy-safe examples.

Lunch or short-cycle club

Advisors use shorter mini-lessons and tightly structured work sessions across several days or weeks.

Best practices: reduce project scope, use templates, assign one task per meeting, and save complex building for longer sessions.

Boot camp or showcase day

Students gather for an intensive experience that combines instruction, design, building, testing, feedback, and presentation.

Best practices: provide pre-work, use stations, pre-approve tools, assign mentors, and keep the prototype challenge focused.

Step 5

Run the semester version

Weeks 1–3: Orientation and five-lesson foundation

Build shared knowledge about human learning, AI literacy, privacy, bias, prompting, and responsible use.

Weeks 4–6: Challenge discovery and task analysis

Conduct interviews, examine work, define the learner, and identify a precise learning challenge.

Weeks 7–8: Strategy research and design

Compare strategies, justify the match, and create an interaction plan.

Weeks 9–11: Build and critique

Create the first prototype, conduct peer critiques, and complete the safety and accessibility review.

Weeks 12–13: User testing

Test with an appropriate user, observe the interaction, and collect structured feedback.

Weeks 14–15: Revision and documentation

Improve the prototype, compare versions, and complete the design notebook and rubric.

Week 16: Showcase and reflection

Present the project, explain evidence and limitations, and identify a possible next phase.

Best for: sustained clubs, elective courses, research-style projects, and programs preparing for a larger showcase or cross-school event.
Weekly meeting routine

A structure that works across formats

  1. Open: review the goal, agenda, and student roles.
  2. Mini-lesson or model: introduce one concept or demonstrate one process.
  3. Work block: students complete a focused project task.
  4. Feedback: teams receive peer or advisor critique.
  5. Documentation: students record decisions, evidence, and contributions.
  6. Close: assign next steps and identify any safety, access, or policy questions.
