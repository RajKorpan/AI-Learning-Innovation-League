  1. What exact problem is being solved?
  2. What must the learner do mentally or physically?
  3. Does AI add genuine value to practice, feedback, adaptation, or simulation?
  4. Could a simpler tool work better?
  5. What privacy, bias, hallucination, accessibility, cost, or account risks appear?

  7. How will support fade?

Possible product types

AI helps design a non-AI tool

Flashcards, memory games, graphic organizers, paper question cards, routines, checklists, or board games.

AI helps produce content

Draft examples, scenarios, practice questions, feedback statements, or variations that students verify and curate.

Conversational AI

A Gem, chatbot, debate partner, reflection coach, question-decomposition coach, or scenario simulator.

Advanced AI development

A custom application in an approved development environment when added complexity serves a testable need.

No AI

A physical manipulative, visual scaffold, card sort, worksheet, routine, or activity may be more reliable and accessible.

Prototype before polishing

Use paper conversation flows, storyboards, role-play, flashcard samples, prompt skeletons, mock transcripts, one-question tests, clickable wireframes, or small sample sets.

Prototype question examples: Does the learner understand what to do? Does the feedback help without giving away the answer? Does the learner stay engaged? Can the teacher-client use the tool without extensive explanation?

Build a Gem

What a Gem is and when to use one

A Gem is a customized version of Gemini that follows reusable instructions. Use one when repeatable conversation adds value and the advisor confirms current account, age, school, privacy, and sharing requirements.

Recommended progression
  1. Use an existing Gem.
  2. Identify strengths and limitations.
  3. Modify one behavior.
  4. Test the modification.
  5. Build a new Gem for the selected problem.
Step-by-step
  1. Sign in to the approved Gemini web account.
  2. Open Gems or Explore Gems and choose New Gem.
  3. Name the Gem for the learning action.
  4. Use the Project Studio Prompt Builder to create instructions.
  5. Add the intended learner, goal, component, bottleneck, principle, desired action, feedback, fading, reflection, limits, and accessibility.
  6. Add example interactions.
  7. Preview and test edge cases.
  8. Revise and record a new version.
  9. Share only through approved settings.
Starter Gem behavior
Ask one question at a time. Require an attempt before help. Use graduated hints: question, cue, partial example. Do not complete the entire task. After each response, identify one accurate idea, one gap, and one next question. At the end, prompt reflection and a next step. Do not request sensitive information or make claims about fixed learning styles.

Custom applications

Use advanced development only when a custom interface, data flow, simulation, or interaction adds clear value. The team still needs empathy evidence, task analysis, learning-science support, low-resolution prototypes, safe test data, accessibility, and version documentation.

Build checkpoint

  • Use age-appropriate language.
  • State tool limits.
  • Protect privacy and minimize data.
  • Preserve learner agency.
  • Avoid inaccessible formats.
  • Provide alternatives.
  • Test answer requests, hallucinations, bias, repeated errors, and frustration.
  • Document versions and unexpected outputs.

Choose the right kind of tool

Five ways AI may—or may not—be involved

AI helps design the tool

Students use AI to brainstorm or draft a non-AI product such as flashcards, memory games, graphic organizers, paper question cards, or a routine.

AI helps produce content

AI generates draft examples, scenarios, practice questions, feedback statements, or variations. Students verify and curate the content.

The tool uses conversational AI

A Gem, chatbot, role-play partner, debate partner, or reflection coach responds to the learner.

Advanced AI development

A custom application is built in Google AI Studio or another approved development environment when a more complex interface or workflow adds value.

The best solution does not use AI

A manipulative, visual scaffold, card sort, worksheet, board game, checklist, or teaching routine may be more reliable and accessible.

Decision guide

  1. What exact problem is being solved?
  2. What must the learner do mentally or physically?
  3. Does AI add genuine value to practice, feedback, adaptation, or simulation?
  4. Could a simpler tool work better?
  5. What privacy, bias, hallucination, accessibility, cost, or account risks does AI introduce?
  6. Can the tool be tested safely and realistically within the project schedule?
  7. Will the support fade, or will the learner become dependent?

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the design stage

  • Protect privacy and avoid collecting unnecessary data.
  • Consider bias and unequal feedback.
  • Preserve learner agency and productive struggle.
  • Include reading, visual, hearing, motor, language, and cognitive-access needs.
  • Plan a low-tech alternative.
  • Do not select a tool because it is technically impressive.
Low-resolution prototype guide

Low-resolution prototype formats

Paper conversation flow

Write learner and tool turns on cards and rearrange the sequence.

Storyboard

Sketch what the learner sees, does, thinks, and receives at each step.

Role-play

One person acts as the tool while another acts as the learner.

Flashcard sample

Create a small set that demonstrates retrieval, feedback, spacing, or elaboration.

Prompt skeleton

Write only the role, sequence, hints, feedback, limits, and reflection rules.

Mock chatbot transcript

Show several possible learner responses and how the tool should adapt.

One-question test

Prototype the hardest or most important interaction rather than the entire product.

Clickable wireframe

Link simple screens without building the underlying system.

Small sample set

Create enough examples, scenarios, or practice items to test the learning strategy.

Every prototype answers a question

  • Does the learner understand what to do?
  • Does the feedback help without giving away the answer?
  • Does the learner stay engaged?
  • Does the tool target the intended component?
  • Can the teacher-client use it without extensive explanation?
  • Does the representation reduce or increase cognitive load?
  • Does AI add value compared with a simpler tool?
Prototype rule: Do not build features that are not needed to answer the current test question.

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the prototyping stage

  • Use non-identifying and non-sensitive sample content.
  • Choose an accessible format and provide alternatives.
  • Do not make the prototype more public than necessary.
  • Label simulated or AI-generated content.
  • Ask what harm, confusion, dependence, or exclusion the prototype could create.
Complete Gem-building guide
Access check: Google’s current instructions say custom Gems are created and edited in the Gemini web app. Personal-account eligibility is generally age 13 or the applicable age in the user’s country; school and work accounts may have different terms and availability. Advisors must verify current requirements before student use.

Recommended progression

  1. Use an existing Gem: experience a premade Gem and document what it asks, tells, adapts, and ignores.
  2. Identify strengths and limitations: evaluate learning strategy, answer-giving, feedback, accessibility, bias, accuracy, and privacy.
  3. Modify one behavior: copy a customizable Gem when available, or use a starter instruction set.
  4. Test the modification: compare the original and revised behavior using the same examples.
  5. Create a new Gem: only after the team can explain the learning challenge, evidence, and intended interaction.

Step-by-step creation

  1. On a computer, sign in to the approved Gemini web account.
  2. Open Gems or Explore Gems, then choose New Gem.
  3. Name the Gem for the learning action—not for a vague subject. Example: “Graph Scale Interval Coach.”
  4. Paste or write the instructions using the Learning-Design Prompt Builder.
  5. Include the intended learner, learning goal, smallest component, current knowledge, bottleneck, learning-science principle, desired action, feedback, fading, reflection, limits, and accessibility.
  6. Add example interactions that show both successful and unsuccessful learner responses.
  7. Use the preview area to test. Previewing does not necessarily save the Gem; save after review.
  8. Test edge cases: requests for answers, irrelevant topics, false claims, sensitive information, repeated errors, inaccessible output, and user frustration.
  9. Revise the instructions and record a new version.
  10. Share only through advisor-approved settings and remove identifying information.

Starter Gem instructions

You are a learning-design practice partner. Your purpose is to help a learner practice one specific component. Begin by asking the learner to explain what they already understand. Ask one question at a time. Require an attempt before giving help. Use graduated hints: first a question, then a cue, then a partial example. Do not complete the entire task. After each response: 1. Identify one accurate idea. 2. Identify one gap or error in reasoning. 3. Ask a focused follow-up question. 4. Give a hint only when needed. 5. Ask the learner to explain what changed. At the end, ask the learner to summarize the strategy used, rate confidence with evidence, and name a next step. Do not request names, school records, health information, account information, or other sensitive data. Acknowledge uncertainty. Encourage verification. Do not describe the learner as having a fixed visual, auditory, or kinesthetic learning style.

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the Gem-building stage

  • Verify current age, account, school, and sharing requirements.
  • Do not upload identifying learner data or unapproved files.
  • Test false claims, answer requests, bias, repeated errors, and sensitive disclosures.
  • Provide a non-AI alternative.
  • Use private or limited sharing unless public access is explicitly approved.
  • Document versions and unexpected outputs.

Accessibility prompts

Reading level

Can the intended learner understand the instructions and feedback?

Visual access

Is contrast readable? Is meaning available without color alone? Are visuals described?

Hearing access

Are captions, transcripts, or text alternatives available?

Motor access

Can the tool be used with keyboard, switch, touch, or alternative input?

Language access

Are key terms defined? Are translations or language supports needed?

Cognitive load

Are steps, choices, and displays manageable without hiding the whole task?

Device availability

Can the tool work on available devices and connections?

Low-tech alternative

Can the same learning goal be reached without the platform?

Stop and involve an advisor when

  • A user shares sensitive personal information.
  • The tool generates harmful, discriminatory, or age-inappropriate output.
  • The project makes medical, psychological, disability, placement, or other high-stakes claims.
  • The platform or sharing setting is not approved.
  • A user becomes distressed or wants to stop.
  • Students cannot explain or verify an important claim.
