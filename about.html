What Is the League?

A human-centered learning-design program built around authentic problems.

Students investigate how teaching and learning break down, connect those challenges to learning-science evidence, create low-cost learning tools, and refine them through interviews, testing, reflection, and public explanation.

The program has three connected components

Classroom curriculum

A five-lesson foundation introduces human learning, AI literacy, empathy interviews, problem definition, task analysis, evidence, prototyping, and responsible testing.

Student-led clubs

Teams work with teacher-clients and peer learners to create one shared learning tool. Clubs may run one-on-one, in small groups, in classrooms, virtually, or through short intensives.

Showcases and competition

Students present empathy findings, learning-science evidence, prototypes, testing, revisions, and educational justification through progressively demanding levels.

The League is not primarily an app-building program

A team begins by understanding people and learning—not by selecting an AI platform. The final product may use AI, may have been designed with AI, or may not use AI at all.

Possible outputExample
Paper or physical toolQuestion cards, manipulatives, flashcards, memory games, card sorts, graphic organizers, checklists
Learning activityDiscussion routine, peer-explanation protocol, reading annotation process, practice sequence
Prompt-based toolStructured prompt used with an approved AI assistant
Conversational AIGem, chatbot, debate partner, reflection coach, question-decomposition coach
Custom applicationA simple website or application when advanced development adds genuine educational value

A continuous cycle of human-centered improvement

  1. Teacher-clients and learners describe authentic needs.
  2. Students identify the smallest meaningful learning component.
  3. Teams research how that component develops and what supports it.
  4. Students imagine multiple tool types rather than committing to the first idea.
  5. Teams build a low-resolution prototype to answer a specific question.
  6. Testing challenges assumptions and may send the team backward.
  7. Students revise and explain what evidence supports the final design.
