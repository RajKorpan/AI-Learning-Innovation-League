What is the League?

The League is a human-centered learning-design program. Students may begin with a learning challenge they experience themselves or one experienced by someone else. Teachers and learners help teams understand authentic needs, test assumptions, and evaluate designs.

The final learning tool does not always use AI. It may be a paper tool, game, flashcards, graphic organizer, learning activity, prompt, Gem, chatbot, or custom application.
AI literacy is part of every League project. Students study human-versus-machine reasoning, prompting, output evaluation, privacy, bias, and responsible use even when the team concludes that the strongest final learning tool should not use AI.

How the League works

Guided League process

Five sessions are organized through a six-stage journey—Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, and Share—and integrate human learning, AI literacy, interviewing, task analysis, evidence, prototyping, testing, and reflection.

Student teams

Students work as a team to create one shared project, whether the project begins with their own learning or someone else’s.

Public sharing

Teams explain their evidence, design decisions, testing, revisions, responsible AI, accessibility, and limitations.

What makes the League distinctive

  • Students begin with authentic learning problems grounded in their own experience, teacher observations, learner experience, or a combination of these.
  • Students use empathy interviews, task analysis, and learning science to investigate the problem.
  • Students may design to help themselves or someone else learn, but they must use evidence rather than assumptions in either case.
  • Students compare AI and non-AI solutions.
  • Testing may require teams to redefine the problem.
  • The League combines guided preparation, student teams, testing, and public sharing.
  • The innovation is the League model—not a single AI platform.

Why human learning comes first

Prior knowledge

New ideas are interpreted through what the learner already knows. A missing prerequisite may be the real bottleneck.

Productive struggle

Appropriate challenge keeps the learner thinking without creating unnecessary frustration.

Check your own thinking

Learners plan, monitor understanding, notice confusion, choose strategies, and evaluate progress.

Plan, check, and adjust

Learners manage goals, attention, time, motivation, help-seeking, and persistence.

Feedback

Useful feedback is specific, connected to reasoning, and actionable without completing the task.

Transfer

Deeper understanding appears when knowledge can be applied in a new problem, context, or subject.

Learning myth check: The League does not classify students as fixed visual, auditory, or kinesthetic learners. Design choices should match the content, task, prior knowledge, specific difficulty, sensory access, accessibility need, and evidence-supported strategy.

Human-versus-machine reasoning

Students compare human understanding, experience, judgment, and accountability with AI pattern generation, fluency, uncertainty, and error.

Computational thinking

Students decompose complex learning tasks, identify patterns and decision points, represent processes, and test whether a proposed sequence works.

Five roles AI may play

Research partner

Generate questions, search terms, candidate explanations, and sources that students verify.

Design assistant

Support brainstorming, scenarios, examples, wording, and critique without making the final decision.

Tool-building platform

Support a Gem, chatbot, or application when conversational or generative behavior adds value.

Delivery mechanism

Guide practice, hints, feedback, role-play, retrieval, or reflection.

Critical object of study

Students examine hallucinations, bias, privacy, answer substitution, and human-versus-machine reasoning.

When AI may not add value

  • A visual, checklist, manipulative, card set, or routine meets the need more clearly.
  • The task requires a human relationship or professional judgment.
  • The platform creates privacy, age, accessibility, cost, or account barriers.
  • The team cannot safely and realistically test the AI behavior.
Flexible by design

The same League process can support different kinds of teams and projects

Every team follows the same core learning-design journey: understand a learner, define a focused problem, choose a learning strategy, build something small, test it, improve it, and share what was learned. Teams can differ in how independently they work and what they choose to build.

Different ways to build

  • Paper, physical, social, or game-based learning experiences
  • AI-assisted materials and activities
  • Reusable conversational AI learning tools
  • More advanced digital builds when the team has the experience

Different ways to participate

  • Small student teams or more independent students
  • More or less facilitator support based on readiness
  • Peer feedback and testing
  • Learning Showcases where teams demonstrate and discuss their work

Hunter College pathways

League participation can help students connect research, learning science, AI literacy, teamwork, communication, and ethical design to college study and future leadership.

Build a high-school foundation

Develop reasoning, writing, mathematics, science, computing, collaboration, and communication.

Use League work as college preparation

Practice research, source evaluation, problem framing, prototyping, testing, revision, and public explanation.

Explore fields of study

Consider computer science, education, psychology, mathematics, data, neuroscience, and related fields.

Connect with Hunter experiences when available

Activities may include campus visits, feedback from faculty or students, research exposure, and near-peer mentoring when available.

Return as a leader

Students may later serve as mentors, reviewers, club leaders, or community partners.

Important: League participation does not guarantee admission to Hunter College.
