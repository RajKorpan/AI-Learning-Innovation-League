The Learning Design Process

Use five connected design-thinking modes and nine student-friendly actions. Each stage includes a product, questions, and a responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint.

Empathize · Understand and Listen

Conduct empathy interviews

An empathy interview is a flexible conversation—not a rigid script. It explores stories, emotions, needs, strategies, barriers, and workarounds before the team chooses a solution.

How to prepare and interview
  1. State what you hope to understand—not the solution you hope to prove.
  2. Prepare five to seven starter questions.
  3. Ask permission to take notes or record.
  4. Begin with a recent, concrete experience.
  5. Follow interesting responses with “Why?”, “Can you tell me more?”, or “Can you show me?”
  6. Listen for stories, emotions, needs, strategies, and workarounds.
  7. End by asking what you missed.
Teacher-client interview

Ask a teacher about something difficult to teach, a prerequisite students often lack, signs of confusion, current supports, and what a short intervention should make possible.

Examples: reading comprehension, breaking down test questions, interpreting a data table, converting units, graphing results, division in science, proportional reasoning, retrieving background knowledge, vocabulary, and managing a complex task.

Peer learner interview

Ask what the learner is trying to do, what they already know, where the process breaks down, what they try next, and what support helps or interferes.

Practice interview

Practice with a teammate or a chatbot role-playing a teacher or learner. A simulation helps you practice follow-up questions but does not replace a real interview.

Avoid leading: Do not ask, “Would a chatbot help?” Ask, “What support would be useful at that moment?”
Define · Diagnose

Select the smallest meaningful component

Too broad

“Students are bad at science.”

Better

“Students struggle to interpret the axes and scale of a graph.”

More precise

“Students can identify the variables but cannot determine the interval represented between labeled points.”

Use the Problem Size Check and Task Analysis in the Project Studio. Identify the final goal, prerequisites, vocabulary, concepts, procedures, decisions, common errors, cognitive-load points, likely bottleneck, and evidence of learning.

Define · Research

Connect the problem to learning science

Investigate skill development, misconceptions, practice, memory, feedback, motivation, metacognition, self-regulation, transfer, prior knowledge, cognitive load, and accessibility.

Evidence ratingMeaning
Strong research supportMultiple credible studies or syntheses support the claim in relevant contexts.
Mixed or limited supportEvidence is incomplete, context dependent, or only partly relevant.
Unsupported claimThe claim lacks a verifiable source or is generated without evidence.
Learning mythA familiar claim—such as matching instruction to fixed learning styles—should not guide design as established science.
Ideate · Imagine

Generate several kinds of learning support

  • AI helps design a non-AI tool.
  • AI helps produce draft examples or content.
  • The tool uses conversational AI.
  • The tool uses advanced custom development.
  • The best solution does not use AI.

Compare what the learner must do, whether AI adds genuine value, whether a simpler tool works better, risks introduced, and whether the team can test the idea safely.

Prototype · Build

Build only enough to learn

Use paper conversation flows, storyboards, role-play, flashcard samples, prompt skeletons, mock transcripts, one-question tests, clickable wireframes, or small sets of sample interactions.

Every prototype answers a question: Does the learner understand what to do? Does feedback help without giving away the answer? Does the learner stay engaged? Does the tool target the intended component?
Test · Test and Improve

Challenge assumptions

  1. Team self-test
  2. Peer-team test
  3. Teacher-client review
  4. Learner test
  5. Revised test

Record what the user tried, where the user hesitated, what was misunderstood or ignored, what worked, what the user said, what the user did, whether the target behavior changed, and what revision follows.

Assume you may be wrong: Testing may reveal that the team must redefine the problem or choose a different strategy.
Reflect

Reflect after every stage

After empathy
  • What surprised us?
  • What assumptions changed?
  • What needs did we hear repeatedly?
After problem definition
  • Why is this the right-sized problem?
  • What evidence supports our definition?
After research
  • What changed our thinking?
  • Which claims are strongest?
After prototyping
  • What question is this prototype testing?
  • What are we deliberately not building?
After testing
  • What worked?
  • What failed?
  • What did users do that we did not expect?
  • Does the problem need to be redefined?
Share

Present an educational justification

Explain the teacher-client need, empathy findings, smallest component, task analysis, learning-science evidence, product choice, productive struggle, feedback and fading, testing, revisions, responsible AI, accessibility, impact, limitations, and team reflection.
