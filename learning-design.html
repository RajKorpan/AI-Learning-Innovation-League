Empathize · Understand and Listen

Conduct empathy interviews

An empathy interview is a flexible conversation—not a rigid script. It explores stories, emotions, needs, strategies, barriers, and workarounds before the team chooses a solution.

How to prepare and interview
  1. State what you hope to understand—not the solution you hope to prove.
  2. Prepare five to seven starter questions.
  3. Ask permission to take notes or record.
  4. Begin with a recent, concrete experience.
  5. Follow interesting responses with “Why?”, “Can you tell me more?”, or “Can you show me?”
  6. Listen for stories, emotions, needs, strategies, and workarounds.
  7. End by asking what you missed.
Teacher-client interview

Ask a teacher about something difficult to teach, a prerequisite students often lack, signs of confusion, current supports, and what a short intervention should make possible.

Examples: reading comprehension, breaking down test questions, interpreting a data table, converting units, graphing results, division in science, proportional reasoning, retrieving background knowledge, vocabulary, and managing a complex task.

Peer learner interview

Ask what the learner is trying to do, what they already know, where the process breaks down, what they try next, and what support helps or interferes.

Practice interview

Practice with a teammate or a chatbot role-playing a teacher or learner. A simulation helps you practice follow-up questions but does not replace a real interview.

Avoid leading: Do not ask, “Would a chatbot help?” Ask, “What support would be useful at that moment?”

Ideate · Imagine

Generate several kinds of learning support

  • AI helps produce draft examples or content.

Prototype · Build

Build only enough to learn

Use paper conversation flows, storyboards, role-play, flashcard samples, prompt skeletons, mock transcripts, one-question tests, clickable wireframes, or small sets of sample interactions.

Every prototype answers a question: Does the learner understand what to do? Does feedback help without giving away the answer? Does the learner stay engaged? Does the tool target the intended component?

Empathy interviews: complete protocols

What makes an empathy interview different?

Empathy interviewSurveyOrdinary discussion
Follows stories and unexpected responses; asks why, asks for examples, and observes context.Uses standardized questions to compare responses across people.May exchange opinions without systematically examining a user’s experience.
Purpose: discover needs and reframe the problem.Purpose: measure patterns.Purpose: discuss or share.

Prepare without writing a rigid script

  1. State what you hope to understand—not the solution you hope to prove.
  2. Prepare five to seven starter questions.
  3. Ask permission to take notes or record.
  4. Begin with a recent, concrete experience.
  5. Follow interesting statements with “Why?”, “Can you tell me more?”, or “Can you show me?”
  6. Listen for stories, emotions, needs, strategies, barriers, contradictions, and workarounds.
  7. End by asking what you missed.

Three interview protocols

Teacher-client interview

Ask about a concept that is difficult to teach, a prerequisite students often lack, signs that learners are confused, current supports, and what a short intervention should make possible.

Starter questions: “Tell us about the last time students got stuck.” “What do successful students do differently?” “Can you show us an example?”

Peer learner interview

Ask what the learner is trying to accomplish, what they already know, where the process breaks down, what they do next, how they feel, and which supports help or interfere.

Starter questions: “Walk us through what you do.” “Where do you hesitate?” “What have you tried?”

Practice interview

Interview a teammate first, or practice with a chatbot role-playing a teacher or learner. A simulation helps students practice follow-up questions but does not replace a real teacher-client or learner interview.

Synthesize findings

  • Separate direct evidence from your interpretation.
  • Group repeated needs, emotions, strategies, and barriers.
  • Identify contradictions rather than averaging them away.
  • Write three to five insight statements.
  • List assumptions that changed.
  • Do not select a solution yet.
Avoid leading questions: Do not ask, “Would a chatbot help you?” Ask, “What support would be useful at that moment?”
Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the empathy stage

  • Obtain permission before notes, recordings, or photographs.
  • Do not collect unnecessary identifying or sensitive information.
  • Use age-appropriate questions and allow the interviewee to skip or stop.
  • Do not use AI-generated role-play as a substitute for real stakeholder input.
  • Store and share notes according to advisor instructions.
Teacher-client problems and problem-size guidance

Teacher-client problem examples

Reading comprehension

Locating the sentence or idea that answers an inference question.

Test-question breakdown

Identifying the action requested by a multi-part question.

Data-table interpretation

Locating the correct row, column, unit, and comparison.

Measurement conversion

Choosing a conversion relationship and checking whether the result is reasonable.

Graphing results

Selecting a scale and determining the interval between labeled points.

Division in science

Recognizing when a scientific quantity represents a ratio or rate.

Proportional reasoning

Distinguishing additive from multiplicative relationships.

Background knowledge

Retrieving a prerequisite fact or concept needed to interpret new information.

Vocabulary and facts

Recalling disciplinary terms and using them accurately in context.

Complex tasks

Planning, sequencing, monitoring, and checking a task with several steps.

From broad to precise

Too broad

“Students are bad at science.”

Better

“Students struggle to interpret the axes and scale of a graph.”

More precise

“Students can identify the variables but cannot determine the interval represented between labeled points.”

Interactive Problem Size Check

Write a problem statement

Template: The teacher-client needs learners to ______. Learners can already ______, but evidence shows they have difficulty ______. The team will focus on the smallest meaningful component ______, and improvement will be visible when learners can ______.
Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the problem-definition stage

  • Describe the difficulty without labeling or diagnosing the learner.
  • Use evidence from interviews, observation, or work—not stereotypes.
  • Remove identifying details from examples.
  • Include accessibility needs as design conditions, not fixed learner identities.
  • Ask whether the problem itself needs to be reframed.
Task analysis: complete guide
Generated task map
Complete the fields and generate a task map.

Select one bottleneck

  • It is necessary for the larger learning goal.
  • It is not merely a symptom of another missing prerequisite.
  • The learner can practice a specific mental or physical action.
  • Improvement can be observed in a short test.
  • A team can prototype a support without solving the entire subject.
Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the task-analysis stage

  • Do not infer disability, motivation, or ability from one error.
  • Separate what the learner cannot yet do from what the learner has not yet been taught.
  • Consider language, sensory access, device access, and cognitive load.
  • Do not upload identifiable work samples to AI tools.
  • Verify AI-generated decompositions with the teacher-client.
Research how people learn

Research questions

Skill development

How does the target knowledge or skill typically develop?

Misconceptions

Which errors are common, and what reasoning may produce them?

Practice

What type, sequence, spacing, or variability of practice is effective?

Memory and retrieval

What must be recalled, recognized, or reconstructed?

Feedback

What feedback is useful, and when should it occur?

Motivation and persistence

What supports agency, relevance, confidence, and recovery after error?

Metacognition and self-regulation

What should learners plan, monitor, reflect on, or change?

Transfer and prior knowledge

What prerequisites and varied contexts support flexible use?

Cognitive load and accessibility

What representations, task chunks, sensory access, or alternatives reduce unnecessary barriers?

Choose the right kind of tool

Five ways AI may—or may not—be involved

AI helps design the tool

Students use AI to brainstorm or draft a non-AI product such as flashcards, memory games, graphic organizers, paper question cards, or a routine.

AI helps produce content

AI generates draft examples, scenarios, practice questions, feedback statements, or variations. Students verify and curate the content.

The tool uses conversational AI

A Gem, chatbot, role-play partner, debate partner, or reflection coach responds to the learner.

Advanced AI development

A custom application is built in Google AI Studio or another approved development environment when a more complex interface or workflow adds value.

The best solution does not use AI

A manipulative, visual scaffold, card sort, worksheet, board game, checklist, or teaching routine may be more reliable and accessible.

Decision guide

  1. What exact problem is being solved?
  2. What must the learner do mentally or physically?
  3. Does AI add genuine value to practice, feedback, adaptation, or simulation?
  4. Could a simpler tool work better?
  5. What privacy, bias, hallucination, accessibility, cost, or account risks does AI introduce?
  6. Can the tool be tested safely and realistically within the project schedule?
  7. Will the support fade, or will the learner become dependent?

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the design stage

  • Protect privacy and avoid collecting unnecessary data.
  • Consider bias and unequal feedback.
  • Preserve learner agency and productive struggle.
  • Include reading, visual, hearing, motor, language, and cognitive-access needs.
  • Plan a low-tech alternative.
  • Do not select a tool because it is technically impressive.
Low-resolution prototyping

Low-resolution prototype formats

Paper conversation flow

Write learner and tool turns on cards and rearrange the sequence.

Storyboard

Sketch what the learner sees, does, thinks, and receives at each step.

Role-play

One person acts as the tool while another acts as the learner.

Flashcard sample

Create a small set that demonstrates retrieval, feedback, spacing, or elaboration.

Prompt skeleton

Write only the role, sequence, hints, feedback, limits, and reflection rules.

Mock chatbot transcript

Show several possible learner responses and how the tool should adapt.

One-question test

Prototype the hardest or most important interaction rather than the entire product.

Clickable wireframe

Link simple screens without building the underlying system.

Small sample set

Create enough examples, scenarios, or practice items to test the learning strategy.

Every prototype answers a question

  • Does the learner understand what to do?
  • Does the feedback help without giving away the answer?
  • Does the learner stay engaged?
  • Does the tool target the intended component?
  • Can the teacher-client use it without extensive explanation?
  • Does the representation reduce or increase cognitive load?
  • Does AI add value compared with a simpler tool?
Prototype rule: Do not build features that are not needed to answer the current test question.

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the prototyping stage

  • Use non-identifying and non-sensitive sample content.
  • Choose an accessible format and provide alternatives.
  • Do not make the prototype more public than necessary.
  • Label simulated or AI-generated content.
  • Ask what harm, confusion, dependence, or exclusion the prototype could create.
Testing and evidence-based revision

Five testing stages

1. Team self-test

Use test cases to identify obvious errors, answer substitution, confusing instructions, and inaccessible interactions.

2. Peer-team test

A different team tries the prototype without inside knowledge and identifies unclear assumptions.

3. Teacher-client review

The teacher checks whether the tool addresses the actual need, fits the context, and uses appropriate content.

4. Learner test

An appropriate learner uses the tool in a realistic context with permission and the option to stop.

5. Revised test

The team tests the changed behavior using comparable tasks and records what improved or remained unresolved.

Required observation record

  • What the user tried to do
  • Where the user hesitated
  • What the user misunderstood
  • What the user ignored
  • What the user used successfully
  • What the user said
  • What the user actually did
  • Whether the target learning behavior changed
  • What unexpected output or emotion appeared
  • What revision follows from the evidence
Assume you may be wrong: The purpose of testing is to challenge—not confirm—the team’s assumptions. A result may require redefining the problem or selecting a different strategy.

Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the testing stage

  • Do not enter identifying student data.
  • Obtain permission and allow users to stop.
  • Test in a realistic but safe context.
  • Observe harm, exclusion, frustration, dependence, and unexpected outputs.
  • Do not coach so much that the prototype appears more usable than it is.
  • Remove identifying details from evidence shared publicly.
Stage-by-stage reflection

Final reflection themes

  • What did we learn about learning?
  • What did we learn about AI?
  • What did we learn about human-centered design?
  • What would we change in the next cycle?
  • How did our team distribute work, disagreement, and leadership?
  • What remains uncertain?
Responsible AI and accessibility checkpoint

Before leaving the reflection stage

  • Do not include identifying details about interviewees or testers.
  • Distinguish evidence from interpretation.
  • Name uncertainty and limitations honestly.
  • Reflect on accessibility, power, and who was not represented.
  • Do not use AI to replace the team’s own reflection.

Accessibility prompts

Reading level

Can the intended learner understand the instructions and feedback?

Visual access

Is contrast readable? Is meaning available without color alone? Are visuals described?

Hearing access

Are captions, transcripts, or text alternatives available?

Motor access

Can the tool be used with keyboard, switch, touch, or alternative input?

Language access

Are key terms defined? Are translations or language supports needed?

Cognitive load

Are steps, choices, and displays manageable without hiding the whole task?

Device availability

Can the tool work on available devices and connections?

Low-tech alternative

Can the same learning goal be reached without the platform?

Stop and involve an advisor when

  • A user shares sensitive personal information.
  • The tool generates harmful, discriminatory, or age-inappropriate output.
  • The project makes medical, psychological, disability, placement, or other high-stakes claims.
  • The platform or sharing setting is not approved.
  • A user becomes distressed or wants to stop.
  • Students cannot explain or verify an important claim.
